# StoryBrand Implementation Guide

> Source: Building a StoryBrand (Donald Miller, 2017), Chapters 12-13 + Marketing Roadmap

## The BrandScript

The StoryBrand BrandScript is a single-page document that captures your entire brand narrative across the 7 SB7 elements. Create it at mystorybrand.com. Every marketing material you produce must derive from this BrandScript. "If you're including messages on your website that don't come from one of the categories of the StoryBrand 7-Part Framework, your customers will only hear noise."

### BrandScript Hierarchy
- **Level 1**: Overall brand BrandScript (one unified message)
- **Level 2**: BrandScript for each division
- **Level 3**: BrandScript for each product within each division
- **Level 4**: BrandScript for each customer segment

Start with Level 1. Everything else is a subplot.

## [SB7:WEB] Building a Better Website

**Source:** Chapter 12

### The 5 Things Your Website MUST Include

Your website is the first impression. It's like a first date. "Pretty websites don't sell things. Words sell things." People don't read websites; they scan them. The days of using a website as a clearinghouse of information are over.

#### [SB7:WEB.1] 1. An Offer Above the Fold

**What it means:** The images and text visible BEFORE scrolling must immediately communicate what you offer.

**Rules:**
- Must be short, enticing, and exclusively customer-centric
- Must meet at least one of these criteria:
  - **Promises an aspirational identity**: "We Will Make You a Pro in the Kitchen!"
  - **Promises to solve a problem**: "Stop Your Cat from Clawing the Furniture"
  - **States exactly what they do**: "We sell clothes. We do hair." (Local Honey)
- Must NOT be: company history, founding story, inside jokes, mission statements, or anything that requires mental effort to decode
- Bold text, easy to read, not buried under buttons and clutter

**Counter-example (bad):** Cooking school website with a carrot cake image, inside joke about Game of Thrones, cartoon video about how the company was founded (Joe met Karen who was friends with Todd...). Customer had to spend 2 hours figuring out the value.

**What it should have said:** "We Will Make You a Pro in the Kitchen!"

**Other examples:**
- Squarespace: "We Help You Make Beautiful Websites"

#### [SB7:WEB.2] 2. Obvious Calls to Action

**Two placements for direct CTA:**
1. **Top right of website** -- the eye moves in a Z pattern (top-left = logo, top-right = CTA)
2. **Center of screen, above the fold** -- offer text followed by CTA button

```
[Logo/Tagline]                    [Buy Now - bright color]

         "Your compelling offer text here"
              [Buy Now - same bright button]
```

**CTA button rules:**
- Different color from all other buttons (preferably brighter, stands out)
- Both buttons must look exactly the same
- Repeat the same button as users scroll down the page
- A person must see something many times before they process it

**Transitional CTA:** Place it in a less-bright button NEXT to the direct CTA. Both "Will you marry me?" and "Can we go out again?" sit side by side.

```
[Buy Now - bright]  [Download Free Guide - less bright]
```

#### [SB7:WEB.3] 3. Images of Success

- Show smiling, happy people who have had a pleasurable experience with your brand
- If you sell products, feature them in the hands of smiling people
- Don't lead with images of your building (unless you're a B&B)
- Not everyone needs to smile (that's inauthentic), but communicate health, well-being, satisfaction
- Images represent an emotional destination the customer wants to reach

#### [SB7:WEB.4] 4. A Bite-Sized Breakdown of Your Revenue Streams

For diverse businesses with multiple offerings:
1. Find an **umbrella message** that unifies all streams
2. Place the umbrella message above the fold
3. As customers scroll down, show 2-3 sections they can choose from
4. Each section links to its own page with its own BrandScript
5. Customers "choose their own adventure" without getting lost

**Example:** Life-planning + corporate strategy facilitation company:
- Umbrella: "The Key to Success Is a Customized Plan"
- Above fold: image of facilitator with client
- Below fold: two sections -- "Personal Life Plans" | "Corporate Strategy Plans"
- Each links to a dedicated page
- Every page includes "Become a Trained Facilitator" button (the real revenue driver)

#### [SB7:WEB.5] 5. Very Few Words

- "People don't read websites anymore; they scan them"
- "Write it in Morse code" -- brief, punchy, relevant
- Most effective websites use **10 sentences or less** on the entire page
- If a paragraph sits above the fold, it's being passed over
- Replace text with images where possible
- Replace paragraphs with 3-4 bullet points
- Use "read more" links for longer explanations
- **Experiment: Can you cut half the words from your website?**
- **Rule: The fewer words you use, the more likely people will read them**

**Bad example:** "As parents ourselves, we understand what it feels like to want the best for our children. That's why we've created a school where parents work closely with teachers through every step of their child's education journey."

**Good example:** Bullet point: "Weekly Conference Calls with Your Child's Teacher" (alongside 5 other differentiators)

### Website as BrandScript Expression

"Think of your StoryBrand BrandScript as a drum kit. Think of your website as a drum solo." Every word, image, and idea must come from your BrandScript. The words don't have to be exact replicas, but the ideas must be the same.

## [SB7:ROAD] The StoryBrand Marketing Roadmap

Five (almost free) things to implement after creating your BrandScript and refining your website.

### [SB7:ROAD.1] Roadmap Task 1: Create a One-Liner

**What it is:** A single statement that replaces "What do you do?" with something that makes people want your business card. More than a slogan -- it's a condensed StoryBrand narrative.

**Inspired by:** Hollywood loglines -- one-sentence movie descriptions that sell screenplays and drive opening weekends.

**Four components:**
1. **The Character** -- who is your customer? (People need to say "That's me!")
2. **The Problem** -- what challenge do they face? (Opens a story loop)
3. **The Plan** -- hint at how you'll solve it (Creates hope)
4. **The Success** -- what life looks like after (Paints the destination)

**Formula assembly:**

| Component | Pilates for moms | Florida time-share |
|-----------|-----------------|-------------------|
| Character | Busy moms | Retired couples |
| Problem | No time to work out | Second mortgage too expensive |
| Plan | Short, meaningful workouts | Time-share option |
| Success | Health and renewed energy | Warm, enjoyable winters |

**Resulting one-liners:**
- "We provide busy moms with a short, meaningful workout they can use to stay healthy and have renewed energy."
- "We help retired couples who want to escape the harsh cold avoid the hassle of a second mortgage while still enjoying the warm, beautiful weather of Florida in the winter."
- StoryBrand's own: "Most business leaders don't know how to talk about their company, so we created a framework that helps them simplify their message, create great marketing material, connect with customers, and grow their business."

**Contrast with bad answers:**
- "I run a gym." (No story, no hook)
- "Well, it's complicated. I got involved in real estate several years ago..." (Noise)

**How to refine:**
- Consider it a rough draft first
- Test repeatedly: friends, spouse, potential customers, strangers at Starbucks
- When people ask for your business card or more information, you've dialed it in

**How to use the one-liner:**
1. **Memorize it** -- recite it as fast as your own name
2. **Have your team memorize it** -- every employee from CEO to groundskeeper. Print on walls, cups, T-shirts. Carry $5 bills and quiz staff daily -- reward correct answers
3. **Include it on your website** -- even a small paragraph. Makes sure the site invites customers into an interesting story
4. **Repeat in EVERY piece of marketing** -- business cards, social media bios, packaging, email signatures, email blasts
5. **Be like James Taylor singing "Fire and Rain"** -- play your hit song over and over with fresh energy every time. Amateurs ramble; professionals serve their audience

### [SB7:ROAD.2] Roadmap Task 2: Create a Lead Generator and Collect Email Addresses

**Why email:** "E-mail is the most valuable and effective way you can spread the word about your business." Social media combined doesn't perform close to email. Email is the most personal digital possession -- getting permission to enter that space is invaluable.

**The newsletter myth:** "Nobody wants to sign up for your newsletter." Newsletter = vague promise of spam. Instead, offer a lead generator -- a specific, valuable resource in exchange for an email address. This is a transitional call to action.

**Two requirements of a lead generator:**
1. Provide enormous value for your customer
2. Establish you as an authority in your field

**Five types of lead generators:**
1. **Downloadable Guide (PDF)** -- cheapest, most effective starting point. Keep to ~3 pages of content. Stuff maximum value in.
   - "5 Mistakes People Make with Their First Million Dollars" (financial advisor)
   - "Building Your Dream Home: 10 Things to Get Right Before You Build" (architect)
2. **Online Course or Webinar** -- more involved but positions you as expert
   - "Becoming a Professional Speaker" (speaking coach)
3. **Software Demo or Free Trial** -- like AOL's 1000-hours CDs in the 90s
4. **Free Samples** -- Blue Apron credits existing customers with free meals to share
5. **Live Events** -- Petco free dog obedience classes; quarterly community classes

**StoryBrand's own lead generator success story:** "5 Things Your Website Should Include" PDF -- 40,000+ downloads. Credited with taking company past $2M. Later: "5-Minute Marketing Makeover" video series took lead gen to next level.

**How much to give away free?** Be as generous as possible. "Give away the 'why,' sell the 'how.'" At StoryBrand: they give away both 'why' AND much of the 'how.' "It's never cost me to be generous."

**List size to see results:** Depends on business, but for <$5M revenue, ~250 qualified email addresses can produce results. A financial advisor might thrive with 500.

**Where to feature:** Prominently on website. Use a pop-up (appears after ~10 seconds -- not immediately). Despite complaints about pop-ups, "stats are clear: they readily outperform nearly every other type of Internet advertising."

### [SB7:ROAD.3] Roadmap Task 3: Create an Automated Email Drip Campaign

**The insight:** Even unopened emails brand you into the customer's consciousness. "Every time one of those customers threw a letter away, even without opening it, our logo was flashing across their eyes." Customers may not need you today or tomorrow, but when they do, they'll remember you exist.

**Nurturing Campaign structure:**
Weekly emails in this pattern:
- Email #1: Nurturing
- Email #2: Nurturing
- Email #3: Nurturing
- Email #4: Sales (with CTA)
- [Repeat cycle]

Write a few months' worth and let it run. Add more over time.

#### [SB7:ROAD.3N] The Nurturing Email Formula

1. Talk about a problem
2. Explain a plan to solve the problem
3. Describe how life can look once the problem is solved
4. Include a P.S. (often the only thing people actually read in a mass email)

Bottom of email: logo, one-liner, phone number.

**Example -- Crest Hill Dog Boarding nurturing email:**
- Subject: "Should We Free Feed Our Dogs?"
- Problem: free feeding can cause health issues
- Plan: feed set amount, once or twice per day, discard after 20 minutes
- Success: dog stays healthy and happy long into their life
- P.S.: "Next time you and your dog are in the shop, introduce us to your dog and we'll tell you everything we know about the breed"

#### [SB7:ROAD.3S] The Sales/Offer Email Formula

1. Talk about a problem
2. Describe a product you offer that solves this problem
3. Describe what life can look like once resolved
4. Call the customer to a direct action leading to a sale

Be DIRECT. "Being passive communicates weakness."

**Example -- Crest Hill Dog Boarding sales email:**
- Subject: "A solution for scary boarding"
- Problem: hate leaving your dog behind, fear of crates and barking
- Product: dogs play all day, sleep eagerly at night, 3 full-time staff, quiet kennels by 8pm
- Offer: book 3 nights at half price (one-time offer to introduce the service)
- CTA: "Call us today at 555-5555"
- P.S.: "The call will only take a couple of minutes, and you'll be in our system forever"

**Email open rates:** 20% is industry standard. Don't worry about low rates. Even deleted emails = branding. Unsubscribers = good (they'd never buy anyway; reduces dead weight on your list).

**Software:** MailChimp (simple, reliable), Infusionsoft (robust, segmentation, advanced).

**Getting started:** Open a Word document and start writing. Paste into email app later. Start small.

### [SB7:ROAD.4] Roadmap Task 4: Collect and Tell Stories of Transformation

**Why:** "People love movies about characters who transform, and they love businesses that help them experience transformation themselves." Testimonials give future customers "the gift of going second."

**The problem with generic testimonials:** "Nancy is a great friend! We highly recommend Nancy!" -- no specific results, no transformation story.

**5 Questions to Generate Great Testimonials:**
1. What was the problem you were having before you discovered our product?
2. What did the frustration feel like as you tried to solve that problem?
3. What was different about our product?
4. Take us to the moment when you realized our product was actually working to solve your problem.
5. Tell us what life looks like now that your problem is solved or being solved.

These questions naturally yield a transformation arc: problem -> frustration -> discovery -> turning point -> new life.

**Use these for:** written testimonials, video testimonials (invite customers for interviews, add b-roll), case studies.

**Where to feature:** emails, promo videos, keynote speeches, live interviews, events, podcasts. StoryBrand closed podcast episodes with transformation interviews -- saw immediate uptick in workshop registrations.

### [SB7:ROAD.5] Roadmap Task 5: Create a System That Generates Referrals

**Why:** "Referrals and peer recommendations are up to 2.5 times more responsive than any other marketing channel" (American Marketing Association).

**Three-step referral system:**

#### Step 1: Identify Existing Ideal Customers
Create a special database of passionate, repeat customers. Communicate with them differently than your general list. Like Domino's "pizza profile" -- builds a relationship for reordering and eventual referral.

#### Step 2: Give Customers a Reason to Spread the Word
DON'T: ask for a list of their friends' email addresses (feels exploitative).
DO: create a valuable, shareable asset (educational video, PDF) that customers can pass along naturally.

**Template referral email:**
> Dear Friend,
> Thanks for doing business with us. We've put together a little video that will help your friends solve [X problem]. If you have any friends with [X problem], feel free to send it along. We'd be happy to follow up.
> We know you value your relationships and so do we.

#### Step 3: Offer a Reward
- Credits/rewards for referrals (Blue Apron gives free meal credits)
- Affiliate program (10% commission on referred orders)

**Real-world referral examples:**
- **100% Refund for 3 Referrals**: Test-prep academy gave full refund after 3 referrals. Parents competed for referrals. Business skyrocketed.
- **Invite-a-Friend Coupons**: Golf range gave each new student coupons for free bucket of balls for a friend. 40% increase in lesson signups.
- **Open-House Party**: Home contractor asked homeowners to host a cookout on their newly built deck (slight discount in exchange). Filled schedule for 12 months with a few parties.
- **Free Follow-Up Photos**: Wedding photographer offered free 1-year anniversary portrait for 3 referrals at wedding time. Followed up with cards to entire bridal party.

**Automation:** Use MailChimp, Infusionsoft, HubSpot, or similar. Include customers who place 1-2 orders in an automated campaign. Opt them out after several orders to avoid annoyance.

## [SB7:CULTURE] Transforming Company Culture with StoryBrand

**Source:** Chapter 13

### The Narrative Void
When there's no story to keep everyone aligned, a "Narrative Void" infects the organization -- splintering it into disconnected factions. Mission statements alone can't fill this void. "Only in very rare cases has a mission *statement* actually led a company to be *on mission*."

### The Thoughtmosphere
An invisible mixture of beliefs and ideas that drives employee behavior and performance. A BrandScript-inspired narrative improves the thoughtmosphere. Every department, every operational detail, every customer experience reinforces the story.

### Impact Example
A billion-dollar restaurant chain went from 5% growth to nearly 30% growth in under 3 years after implementing StoryBrand narrative across the organization -- with the same employees. "For a billion-dollar company, that translated into hundreds of millions per year."

### Employee as Hero
Just as customers are heroes, employees are heroes too:
- External BrandScript: customer = hero, company = guide
- Internal BrandScript: employee = hero, company leadership = guide
- Compensation, development, events = "tools" leadership creates to help employees win

### Implementation Process
1. Create a BrandScript with leadership team
2. Audit the existing thoughtmosphere
3. Create a custom On-Mission implementation plan
4. Optimize internal communications to support the plan
5. Install a self-sustained team for the On-Mission plan

### What On-Mission Companies Do Differently
- Onboarding is an adoption experience, not a checkbox
- New hires learn the customer's story before the company's
- CEO delivers keynote based on BrandScript at new-hire luncheons
- Customer pictures on walls celebrating their successes
- Daily tasks are specific objectives tied to helping customers
- Coworkers are a supportive community, not competition
- The #1 job of an executive: remind stakeholders what the mission is, over and over

## Quick Reference: The Grunt Test

Could a caveman look at your website and immediately grunt:
1. What you offer
2. How it will make their life better
3. What they need to do to buy it

If the answer to any of these is unclear within 5 seconds, you're losing customers.

## [SB7:WIRE] Website Wireframe Template

Based on SB7 principles, a StoryBrand website follows this scroll-down structure:

### Section 1: Above the Fold (First Date)
- **Header**: Logo (top-left) + Direct CTA button (top-right, bright color)
- **Hero area**: Large image of happy customer or aspirational scenario
- **Headline**: Clear offer text (aspirational identity, problem solution, or what you do)
- **Sub-headline**: 1 short sentence expanding the offer
- **CTA buttons**: Direct CTA (bright) + Transitional CTA (less bright, next to it)

### Section 2: The Stakes (Second Date)
- **Problem agitation**: 2-3 short statements about what the customer is struggling with
- **Empathy**: Show you understand their pain (internal problem focus)
- **Failure preview**: Brief mention of what happens if they don't act

### Section 3: The Value Proposition (Third Date)
- **How it works**: 3-step process plan with icons/numbers
- **Success vision**: What life looks like after (images of happy outcomes)
- **Authority proof**: Testimonials (3 max), stats, logos, awards

### Section 4: The Close
- **One-liner**: Your complete brand statement
- **Direct CTA**: Repeated bright button
- **Transitional CTA**: Lead generator offer
- **Footer**: Basic info, links, contact

### Key Wireframe Rules
- Eye moves in Z pattern: top-left -> top-right -> bottom-left -> bottom-right
- Direct CTA appears minimum 3 times on the page
- Every section connects back to BrandScript elements
- No paragraph above the fold -- only bold, short statements
- Images show people experiencing success, not your building or products alone

## [SB7:ELEV] Elevator Pitch Framework

The elevator pitch follows the same structure as the one-liner but expanded slightly for verbal delivery (30-60 seconds):

1. **Hook** (Character + Problem): "You know how [type of customer] struggle with [problem]?"
2. **Bridge** (Guide + Plan): "What we do is [your solution] through [simple plan]."
3. **Payoff** (Success): "So that [customer] can [success outcome]."
4. **CTA**: "If that sounds helpful, [specific next step]."

**Example for a financial advisor:**
"You know how most people feel confused and overwhelmed when it comes to planning for retirement? What we do is create a simple, customized plan that maps out exactly how much you need and how to get there. So that when retirement comes, you can actually enjoy it without worrying about money running out. If that sounds like something you need, I'd love to set up a 30-minute call."

The elevator pitch should be as natural as introducing yourself. Practice until it flows without thinking.

## [SB7:SALES] Sales Script Structure

For phone or in-person sales conversations, the SB7 Framework provides a natural conversation flow:

1. **Open with empathy** (S3.E): "I understand you're dealing with [problem]. That can be really frustrating."
2. **Identify the internal problem** (S2.I): Ask how the problem makes them feel. Listen.
3. **Position as guide** (S3): Share brief authority proof (1-2 sentences about results you've gotten for similar clients)
4. **Present the plan** (S4): "Here's how we typically help people in your situation..." (3 steps)
5. **Paint the success picture** (S7): "What our clients typically experience after working with us is..."
6. **Lightly mention stakes** (S6): "And what we've found is that when people wait too long, [negative consequence]"
7. **Call to action** (S5.D): "The best next step would be to [specific action]. Can we set that up?"

### Sales Conversation Anti-Patterns
- DON'T start by talking about your company history
- DON'T lead with features and specifications
- DON'T overwhelm with too many options
- DON'T be passive about asking for the sale
- DON'T forget to address the internal problem (this is where the buying decision lives)

## [SB7:SOCIAL] Social Media Application

While the book doesn't extensively cover social media, the SB7 principles apply directly:

### Content Strategy Through SB7 Lens
- **Character posts**: Content that reflects your customer's identity and desires
- **Problem posts**: Talk about the frustrations your customers face (especially internal)
- **Guide posts**: Share expertise, tips, and helpful information (empathy + authority)
- **Plan posts**: Break down your process into simple, shareable steps
- **CTA posts**: Direct invitations to engage (link to lead generator or offer)
- **Failure posts**: Highlight what happens when the problem isn't addressed
- **Success posts**: Customer transformations, testimonials, before/after stories

### Social Media Ratio (derived from SB7)
- 40% value/guide content (positions you as guide)
- 30% problem/empathy content (deepens connection)
- 20% success/transformation stories (inspires action)
- 10% direct CTA/offers (asks for the sale)

### Profile Optimization
- Bio = your one-liner `[SB7:ROAD.1]`
- Profile image = you (guide), not your logo (unless major brand)
- Pinned post = your strongest transitional CTA (lead generator)
- Link in bio = landing page that passes the grunt test

## [SB7:KEYNOTE] Keynote/Presentation Framework

Miller's framework works for presentations as well as marketing:

1. **Open with the audience's problem** (not your credentials)
2. **Agitate the internal frustration** (make them feel it)
3. **Position yourself as the guide** (brief credibility, then empathy)
4. **Present your plan/framework** (the meat of the talk -- 3-5 points)
5. **Show what success looks like** (case studies, transformation stories)
6. **Mention what failure looks like** (moderate stakes)
7. **Close with a clear call to action** (specific next step)

### Keynote Anti-Patterns (from the book)
- Starting with "Let me tell you about my company..."
- Leading with credentials and awards
- Covering too many topics (each talk = one BrandScript)
- Ending without a clear CTA
- Making yourself the hero of every story you tell

## [SB7:LANDING] Landing Page Optimization

A dedicated landing page (for a specific product/campaign) follows a tighter version of the website wireframe:

### Essential Elements
1. **Headline**: addresses specific external + internal problem
2. **Sub-headline**: hints at the solution
3. **Hero image**: customer in the aspirational state
4. **3-step process plan**: visual, numbered, simple
5. **Social proof**: 2-3 targeted testimonials
6. **Stakes section**: brief failure mention (what you lose by NOT acting)
7. **Success section**: specific outcomes (bullet points, not paragraphs)
8. **Primary CTA**: repeated 2-3 times, always visible
9. **Risk reversal**: guarantee, agreement plan, or trial offer
10. **Scarcity/urgency**: if authentic (limited time, limited spots)

### Landing Page Copy Formula
- **Headline**: [Aspirational identity] OR [Problem you solve]
- **Sub-headline**: [How you do it in one sentence]
- **Body bullets**: 3-5 specific outcomes (not features)
- **CTA button text**: Active verb + desired result ("Get My Free Guide," "Start Saving Today")

## [SB7:PRINT] Print and Physical Materials

The SB7 Framework applies to physical marketing too:

### Business Cards
- Front: Name, title, one-liner
- Back: Direct CTA (URL/phone) + Transitional CTA (lead generator URL)

### Brochures / Flyers
- Front panel: Offer above the fold (headline + image of success)
- Inside: 3-step plan + testimonials + failure/success contrast
- Back panel: Direct CTA + contact info

### Signage / Banners
- Maximum 7-10 words
- Must answer: what do you offer? How does it help me?
- Include CTA (URL, phone, QR code)

### Packaging
- Feature one-liner on packaging
- Include CTA for repeat purchase or referral
- Show success imagery (happy customer, not just product)

## Quick Reference: BrandScript Brainstorming Questions

Use these questions when helping a team fill out their BrandScript:

### Character (S1)
- What does our customer want as it relates to our brand?
- Can we state it in one simple sentence?
- Is this desire connected to their survival (money, time, status, meaning)?

### Problem (S2)
- What villain is our brand standing against?
- What is the external problem our brand resolves?
- How does that external problem make our customers feel? (internal)
- Why is it unjust that our customers suffer from this problem? (philosophical)

### Guide (S3)
- Can we express empathy by showing we understand our customer's frustration?
- Do we have testimonials, statistics, awards, or logos that demonstrate competence?

### Plan (S4)
- What are the 3-4 simple steps a customer takes to do business with us?
- What fears or concerns do our customers have about our industry?
- What agreements can we make to alleviate those fears?
- What should we name our plan?

### Call to Action (S5)
- What is our direct call to action? (Buy Now, Schedule, Register, etc.)
- What transitional call to action can we offer? (PDF, webinar, free trial, etc.)
- Is our direct CTA repeated and visually prominent?

### Failure (S6)
- What negative consequences are we helping customers avoid?
- Can we describe a tragic scene they might experience without our product?
- Are we using moderate (not extreme) fear language?

### Success (S7)
- What does our customer's life look like after they use our product?
- What external resolution do we offer?
- How will our customers feel after resolution? (internal)
- How does our product contribute to making the world more just? (philosophical)
- Who does our customer want to become? (aspirational identity: From -> To)
