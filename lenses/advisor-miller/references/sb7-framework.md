# SB7 Framework -- Complete Reference

> Source: Building a StoryBrand: Clarify Your Message So Customers Will Listen (Donald Miller, 2017)

## Central Thesis

"Your customer should be the hero of the story, not your brand." Every phenomenally successful business understands this. The SB7 Framework organizes brand messaging around seven universal story elements that the human brain is wired to follow. "If you confuse, you lose."

## The SB7 Formula (Summary)

**A CHARACTER (1) who has a PROBLEM (2) meets a GUIDE (3) who gives them a PLAN (4) and CALLS THEM TO ACTION (5). That action helps them avoid FAILURE (6) and ends in SUCCESS (7).**

---

## [SB7:S1] Step 1: A Character (The Hero = Customer)

**Source:** Building a StoryBrand (Miller), Chapter 4
**Core Principle:** The customer is the hero of the story, not your brand. When we position our customer as the hero and ourselves as the guide, we will be recognized as a trusted resource. A story doesn't pick up until the hero needs to disarm a bomb, win someone's heart, defeat a villain, or fight for survival. Similarly, a brand must define something specific the customer wants -- this opens a "story gap" in the customer's mind that creates interest and engagement.

**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: defining your brand's core message, creating website copy, crafting elevator pitches, writing any marketing material
- AVOID WHEN: you are tempted to talk about your company's founding story, internal goals, or your own journey as the hero
- COMBINES WITH: [SB7:S2] (the character must encounter a problem), [SB7:S7] (the character must see a desirable resolution)

**Key Examples:**
- High-end resort: "Find the luxury and rest you've been looking for" (not: "Our resort was founded in 1985...")
- Financial Advisor: "A Plan for Your Retirement"
- University: "A hassle-free MBA you can complete after work"
- Landscaping company: "A yard that looks better than your neighbor's"
- Caterer: "A mobile fine-dining experience in the environment of your choice"
- Bookstore: "A Story to Get Lost In"
- Breakfast Bars: "A Healthy Start to Your Day"

**Actionable Framework:**
1. Define ONE specific desire your customer has (related to your brand)
2. Make sure that desire connects to survival (physical, emotional, relational, or spiritual)
3. State it simply -- the customer must immediately understand what you offer
4. Use it to "open a story gap" -- customers should wonder if you can help them get what they want

### [SB7:S1.D] Sub-technique: Single Focus Desire
Pare down the customer's ambition to a SINGLE focus. "Can't we mention all twenty-seven things?" No. Multiple story gaps confuse the audience. At the overall brand level, identify one desire. Use sub-BrandScripts for divisions and individual products to address additional desires.

### [SB7:S1.S] Sub-technique: Survival Connection
The desire must connect to survival at a primitive level. Categories of survival desires:
- **Conserving financial resources** (save money -- e.g., Walmart: "Save Money. Live Better")
- **Conserving time** (opportunity costs -- e.g., housecleaning service giving back time)
- **Building social networks** (community, tribe, nurturing -- e.g., connecting to allies)
- **Gaining status** (luxury brands like Mercedes, Rolex -- status as survival mechanism)
- **Accumulating resources** (making money, increasing productivity -- B2B offerings)
- **The innate desire to be generous** (sacrifice, altruism -- part of survival mechanism)
- **The desire for meaning** (Viktor Frankl: chief desire of man is meaning, not pleasure)

### [SB7:S1.G] Sub-technique: Story Gap
A story gap is the distance between a character and what they want. Opening a story gap creates magnetic interest. The brain seeks closure (like the final note of "Twinkle Twinkle Little Star"). When we define what the customer wants, we open a story gap. When they engage our product, the gap closes. This cycle drives human behavior.

**Common Mistake:** Making the brand the hero of the story. Talking about company history, awards, internal culture, or founders when the customer wants to know "where are you taking ME?" Brands that position themselves as heroes unknowingly compete with their customers.

**Reversal:** If your product is truly commodity-level and identical to competitors, the character desire alone won't differentiate. You must combine with internal problem ([SB7:S2.I]) to create emotional resonance that distinguishes your brand.

## [SB7:S2] Step 2: Has a Problem

**Source:** Building a StoryBrand (Miller), Chapter 5
**Core Principle:** Companies tend to sell solutions to external problems, but customers buy solutions to internal problems. There are THREE levels of problems customers face: External, Internal, and Philosophical. A villain causes all three. Identifying and addressing all three levels creates the deepest customer engagement and transforms casual buyers into brand evangelists. "The more we talk about the problems our customers face, the more interest they will have in our brand."

**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: creating any marketing message, writing sales copy, defining your brand promise, developing ad campaigns
- AVOID WHEN: you have already resolved the conflict (post-purchase nurturing should shift to success imagery)
- COMBINES WITH: [SB7:S1] (the character's desire is threatened by the problem), [SB7:S6] (failure = what happens if the problem wins), [SB7:S7] (success = resolution of all three problem levels)

### [SB7:S2.V] Sub-technique: The Villain
Every story needs a villain. The villain is the root source of the customer's problems. Four characteristics of a good villain:
1. **Root source** -- not a feeling (frustration is not a villain; high taxes ARE a villain)
2. **Relatable** -- people immediately recognize it as something they disdain
3. **Singular** -- one villain is enough; too many villains = lack of clarity
4. **Real** -- never fearmonger; fight actual villains on behalf of customers

Examples: Distractions (time-management software), dust bunnies (cleaning products), used-car salesmen (CarMax)

### [SB7:S2.E] Sub-technique: External Problems
The tangible, physical, visible problem the hero must overcome. In stories: a ticking bomb, a runaway bus, a baseball team that can't win. In business: the literal thing your product or service fixes.

Examples:
- Restaurant: hunger
- Plumber: leaky pipe
- Pest control: termites in the attic
- Tesla: "I need a car"
- Nespresso: "I want better-tasting coffee at home"
- Edward Jones: "I need investment help"

### [SB7:S2.I] Sub-technique: Internal Problems
The emotional frustration, self-doubt, or insecurity caused by the external problem. This is where customers actually make buying decisions. "Companies tend to sell solutions to external problems, but people buy solutions to internal problems."

In stories, the hero struggles with: "Do I have what it takes?" -- this universal question makes any story relatable across demographics.

Examples:
- Apple: customers felt INTIMIDATED by computers (internal). Apple solved this with simple, hip interface = massive growth
- National Car Rental: frustration with forced small talk at rental counters
- CarMax: FEAR of being lied to, cheated, or worked over by used-car salesmen
- Starbucks: desire for sophistication and belonging (turned 50-cent coffee into $4 experience)
- House painter: "Paint That Will Make Your Neighbors Jealous" (addresses embarrassment)
- Tesla: "I want to be an early adopter of new technology"
- Nespresso: "I want my home coffee machine to make me feel sophisticated"

### [SB7:S2.P] Sub-technique: Philosophical Problems
The "why does this story matter?" question. Uses terms like "ought" and "shouldn't." Connects the customer's purchase to something larger than themselves.

Examples:
- Tommy Boy: "Bad people shouldn't be allowed to win" (honesty vs. deception)
- The King's Speech: good vs. evil (unifying Britain against Nazis)
- Jerry Maguire: "People are worth more than the money they bring in"
- Pet store: "Pets deserve to eat healthy food too"
- Travel agent: "Because this summer should be remembered forever"
- Tower Records: "No music, no life"
- Tesla: "My choice of car ought to help save the environment"
- Nespresso: "I shouldn't have to be a barista to make a gourmet coffee at home"
- Edward Jones: "If I'm going to invest my money, I deserve an advisor who will thoughtfully explain things in person"

### [SB7:S2.C] Sub-technique: The Perfect Brand Promise (Climactic Resolution)
When a product resolves all three levels -- external, internal, and philosophical -- simultaneously, the customer experiences deep satisfaction. This is the "climactic scene" -- like Luke destroying the Death Star (external: destroy weapon, internal: prove he's a Jedi, philosophical: good defeats evil). Position the "Buy Now" button as the action that creates closure at all three levels.

**Actionable Framework:**
1. Identify ONE villain your brand fights
2. Define the external problem that villain causes
3. Identify the internal frustration that external problem creates
4. Articulate the philosophical injustice ("People ought to..." / "Nobody should have to...")
5. Position your product as the resolution to all three

**Common Mistake:** Talking ONLY about external problems. Most brands do this. "We sell insurance." "We make software." This misses the deeper motivators. Instead: "Nobody should feel overwhelmed by surprise expenses" (philosophical + internal).

**Reversal:** Over-dramatizing problems for trivial products feels inauthentic. A candy bar doesn't need a philosophical treatise. Scale the depth of problem-language to the significance of the purchase.

## [SB7:S3] Step 3: And Meets a Guide

**Source:** Building a StoryBrand (Miller), Chapter 6
**Core Principle:** Customers aren't looking for another hero; they're looking for a guide. The guide has two essential characteristics: EMPATHY and AUTHORITY. Both are required -- one without the other fails. The guide has "been there and done that" and has already conquered the hero's challenge. Position your brand as the guide (like Yoda, Haymitch, Obi-Wan), never as the hero.

**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: writing "About Us" sections, crafting brand voice, creating testimonial strategy, developing trust signals
- AVOID WHEN: you are tempted to make the story about your company's achievements, founding, or internal culture
- COMBINES WITH: [SB7:S1] (the hero needs a guide because they can't solve the problem alone), [SB7:S4] (the guide gives a plan), [SB7:S5] (the guide calls to action)

### [SB7:S3.E] Sub-technique: Express Empathy
Empathy creates trust. Customers trust those who understand them. Oprah Winfrey: "The three things every human being wants most are to be seen, heard, and understood."

Empathetic statements:
- "We understand how it feels to..."
- "Nobody should have to experience..."
- "Like you, we are frustrated by..."
- "We care about your [thing customer values]" (e.g., Toyota: "We care about your Toyota")

Deeper empathy = commonality. When customers realize they have something in common with a brand, they "batch" their thinking and fill unknowns with trust. Example: Discover Card campaign showing customers talking to exact replicas of themselves -- "We'll take care of you the same way you would take care of yourself."

### [SB7:S3.A] Sub-technique: Demonstrate Authority
Authority = competence, NOT superiority. "Nobody likes a know-it-all." The guide doesn't brag but demonstrates proven ability. The nutritionist who says "Me too!" when you want to lose 30 pounds is the WRONG guide.

Four ways to demonstrate authority without bragging:
1. **Testimonials** -- let others do the talking. Keep them brief (not heaps of praise). Three is ideal. Avoid stacking 10-20 (that positions you as hero).
2. **Statistics** -- "125,000 users trust our software" (Infusionsoft). Scratches the left-brain itch.
3. **Awards** -- small logos at the bottom of your page. No big deal needed.
4. **Logos** -- for B2B: place logos of known clients in marketing. Social proof.

### [SB7:S3.F] Sub-technique: First Impression (Trust + Respect)
Harvard professor Amy Cuddy's research: two subconscious questions when meeting someone new:
1. "Can I TRUST this person?" (answered by empathy)
2. "Can I RESPECT this person?" (answered by demonstrated authority/competence)

Trust must come first. Only after trust is established will a person consider the relationship further.

**Actionable Framework:**
1. Write 2-3 empathetic statements showing you understand the customer's internal problem
2. Choose 2-4 authority proof points (testimonials, stats, awards, logos)
3. Place empathy prominently (customers encounter trust signals first)
4. Place authority as supporting evidence (not the main message)
5. Never position your brand as the hero -- keep focus on the customer's journey

**Key Examples:**
- Bill Clinton: "I feel your pain" -- positioned himself as guide to American voters (empathy)
- Yoda: understands Luke's dilemma AND has mastered the Force (empathy + authority)
- Jay Z's Tidal: FAILED because he positioned artists as heroes, not customers. Asked "respect music" instead of "how are we helping YOU win the day?"

**Common Mistake:** Expressing empathy WITHOUT authority (the "Me too!" nutritionist) or demonstrating authority WITHOUT empathy (the condescending expert). Both modes fail independently. You must have the one-two punch.

**Reversal:** In industries where authority is the primary concern (medical, legal, engineering), empathy may need to be more subtle -- a brief acknowledgment rather than extensive emotional language. But never skip it entirely.

## [SB7:S4] Step 4: Who Gives Them a Plan

**Source:** Building a StoryBrand (Miller), Chapter 7
**Core Principle:** Customers trust a guide who has a plan. Even after you've identified what the customer wants, defined their problems, and positioned yourself as guide, they STILL won't buy without a clear plan. Making a purchase is a commitment with risk. The plan is like stepping stones across a rushing creek -- it removes confusion and reduces perceived risk. "If you confuse, you lose" applies here especially: not having a plan is a guaranteed way to confuse customers.

**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: creating purchase flows, designing landing pages, writing "how it works" sections, onboarding sequences
- AVOID WHEN: the process genuinely has only one step (just use a strong CTA instead)
- COMBINES WITH: [SB7:S3] (the guide provides the plan), [SB7:S5] (the plan leads to a call to action)

### [SB7:S4.P] Sub-technique: Process Plan
Describes the steps a customer takes to (a) buy your product, (b) use your product after buying, or (c) both.

**Pre-purchase process plan example:**
1. Schedule an appointment
2. Allow us to create a customized plan
3. Let's execute the plan together

**Post-purchase process plan example:**
1. Download the software
2. Integrate your database into our system
3. Revolutionize your customer interaction

**Combined pre- and post-purchase example:**
1. Test-drive a car
2. Purchase the car
3. Enjoy free maintenance for life

Rules:
- Minimum 3 steps, maximum 6
- If the real process has 20-30 steps, group them into phases
- Studies show: more info = fewer purchases. Simplify.
- The goal: customer thinks "Oh, I can do that. That's not hard."

### [SB7:S4.A] Sub-technique: Agreement Plan
If process plans alleviate CONFUSION, agreement plans alleviate FEARS. An agreement plan is a list of promises you make to customers to overcome their fear of doing business with you.

Examples:
- CarMax: no haggling, quality certification, non-commissioned salespeople, renewal process
- Newt Gingrich's "Contract with America": turned talking points into a list of agreements with voters
- Whole Foods: list of values = agreement to source food responsibly

How to create:
1. List all fears/concerns customers have about your product/service/industry
2. Create agreements that counter each fear
3. Give the plan a name (increases perceived value)

### [SB7:S4.N] Sub-technique: Name Your Plan
Give your process plan or agreement plan a branded title:
- "Easy Installation Plan"
- "World's Best Night's Sleep Plan"
- "Customer Satisfaction Agreement"
- "Our Quality Guarantee"

Naming increases perceived value and frames the plan in the customer's mind.

**Actionable Framework:**
1. Create a 3-4 step process plan (pre-purchase, post-purchase, or combined)
2. Identify customer fears related to your industry and create an agreement plan
3. Give each plan a compelling name
4. Feature the process plan prominently on your website and marketing
5. Use the agreement plan in supporting materials (may work in background)

**Common Mistake:** Overcomplicating the plan with too many steps. More than 6 steps adds confusion rather than removing it. Also: having no plan at all -- customers standing at the edge of a creek hearing a waterfall downstream won't jump without visible stepping stones.

**Reversal:** For extremely simple, low-cost impulse purchases (candy, basic consumables), an elaborate plan may be unnecessary and could even slow down the buying process. Keep it proportional to the purchase complexity.

## [SB7:S5] Step 5: And Calls Them to Action

**Source:** Building a StoryBrand (Miller), Chapter 8
**Core Principle:** Customers do not take action unless they are challenged to take action. In stories, characters never act on their own -- they must be challenged by outside forces. The same is true in business. Most brands are far too passive with their calls to action. "When we try to sell passively, we communicate a lack of belief in our product." Two types of CTAs work together: Direct (marriage proposal) and Transitional (date invitation).

**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: designing every piece of marketing material -- websites, emails, ads, keynotes, business cards, email signatures
- AVOID WHEN: never. Every piece of communication needs a call to action
- COMBINES WITH: [SB7:S4] (the plan leads to the CTA), [SB7:S6] (stakes make the CTA urgent), [SB7:S7] (success makes the CTA desirable)

### [SB7:S5.D] Sub-technique: Direct Call to Action
Leads directly to a sale or the first step toward a sale. Must be obvious, repeated, and bold.

Examples:
- "Buy Now"
- "Schedule an Appointment"
- "Call Today"
- "Register Today"
- "Order Now"

Placement rules:
- Top right corner of website (different, brighter color)
- Center of screen, above the fold
- Repeated as user scrolls down the page
- In every email blast, on signage, in radio/TV ads
- In team members' email signatures
- On business cards

The "Buy Now" button should be the most prominent, visually distinct element. Customer's eye moves in a Z pattern: top-left (logo) -> top-right (Buy Now) -> center (offer + Buy Now again).

Key insight: "The fastest way to grow a company is to make the calls to action clear and then repeat them over and over."

### [SB7:S5.T] Sub-technique: Transitional Call to Action
Contains less risk. Offers something free. Deepens the relationship until the customer is ready to buy. Like asking someone on a date before proposing marriage.

Three powers of a transitional CTA:
1. **Stakes a claim to your territory** -- positions you as expert in your space
2. **Creates reciprocity** -- generosity creates give-and-take; more you give, more customers give back
3. **Positions you as the guide** -- solving problems (even for free) = guide status

Types of transitional CTAs:
- **Free information**: white paper, PDF, educational video, podcast, webinar, live event
- **Testimonials**: video or PDF with happy client stories
- **Samples**: test drives, tastings, free chapters, music samples
- **Free trial**: limited-time free access (removes risk)

The "dating" metaphor:
- Us: "Will you marry me?" (Direct CTA)
- Customer: "No."
- Us: "Will you go out with me again?" (Transitional CTA)
- Customer: "Yes."
- [Repeat cycle until customer says "Okay, I'll marry you now."]

**Key Example:** StoryBrand grew to multimillion-dollar company in year two using ONLY a transitional CTA: "5 Things Your Website Should Include" PDF. Thousands downloaded it. Back of PDF advertised the workshop. Revenue doubled without spending on marketing.

**Actionable Framework:**
1. Define ONE clear direct call to action
2. Make it visually prominent (different color, larger, bolder)
3. Repeat it multiple times on every page/material
4. Create at least one transitional CTA (PDF, video, webinar)
5. Place transitional CTA alongside direct CTA (less-bright button, secondary position)
6. Remember: "People don't have ESP. They can't read our minds."

**Common Mistake:** Being too passive. "Coffee is nice, isn't it? Do you like coffee?" is not how you get a date -- or a sale. Most brands think they're overselling when they're actually whispering. Of thousands of StoryBrand clients, "we've yet to encounter anybody who oversells."

**Reversal:** In high-trust professional services (legal, medical), aggressive direct CTAs like "Buy Now" may feel inappropriate. Adapt the language ("Schedule a Consultation," "Book Your Assessment") but maintain the boldness and repetition.

## [SB7:S6] Step 6: That Helps Them Avoid Failure

**Source:** Building a StoryBrand (Miller), Chapter 9
**Core Principle:** Every human being is trying to avoid a tragic ending. Stories live and die on a single question: "What's at stake?" If nothing can be gained or lost, nobody cares. Brands must show customers the cost of NOT doing business with them. This is NOT fearmongering -- it's defining stakes. "If there are no stakes, there is no story." Failure is like salt in a recipe: too much ruins the flavor, but leaving it out makes the recipe bland.

**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: creating urgency in marketing, writing email campaigns, defining the "so what" of your product, crafting blog posts
- AVOID WHEN: you've already established strong stakes and are in danger of overdoing it (salt metaphor)
- COMBINES WITH: [SB7:S2] (the problem creates the potential for failure), [SB7:S5] (the CTA is how to avoid failure), [SB7:S7] (success = the opposite of failure)

### [SB7:S6.L] Sub-technique: Loss Aversion
Based on Daniel Kahneman's Prospect Theory (1979, Nobel Prize): people are 2-3x more motivated to avoid loss than to achieve gain. Losing $100 causes more dissatisfaction than winning $100 causes satisfaction. This means loss-framing in marketing is a powerful motivator.

Example: Lyndon Johnson telling George Wallace his legacy would be tarnished if he didn't support the Civil Rights Act. "Either they'd build a statue in his honor or he'd be remembered for instigating hate."

### [SB7:S6.F] Sub-technique: Fear Appeal (4-Step Process)
From Infante, Rancer, and Womack's "Building Communication Theory":
1. **Establish vulnerability**: "Nearly 30 percent of all homes have evidence of termite infestation"
2. **Call to reduce vulnerability**: "Since nobody wants termites, you should do something about it"
3. **Offer specific solution**: "We offer a complete home treatment that will insure your house is free of termites"
4. **Challenge to take action**: "Call us today and schedule your home treatment"

Key calibration: moderate fear is most effective. High fear = people block it out. Low fear = too weak to motivate. Moderate fear = attitude and behavior change.

### [SB7:S6.S] Sub-technique: Define the Stakes
Brainstorm what negative consequences customers avoid by using your product:
- Could they lose money?
- Are there health risks?
- What about opportunity costs?
- Could their quality of life decline?
- What's the cost of not doing business with you?

**Key Examples:**
- Allstate "Mayhem" campaign: Dean Winters portraying everything from raccoons to fires. Project Share Aware: criminals burglarizing homes while you post on social media. Result: 18M hits, #1 trending worldwide.
- Perkins Motorplex (used cars): "Getting ripped off," "Being stuck with a lemon," "Feeling taken advantage of"
- Rely Technology (home audio/video): "Living in a boring home," "Nobody will want to watch the game at your house," "You need a PhD to turn on the TV"
- Win Shape Camps (summer camp): "A long, boring summer," "A bunch of restless kids in your house," "Regret about having wasted the summer"
- Financial advisor: "Don't postpone your retirement. You've worked too hard for too long to not enjoy time with your grandchildren"

**Actionable Framework:**
1. List 3-5 negative consequences your customers face without your product
2. Use moderate fear language (not apocalyptic, not trivial)
3. Include these stakes in blog subjects, email content, and bullet points on website
4. Keep it brief in the BrandScript (only a few bullet points needed)
5. Pair every failure element with the success alternative

**Common Mistake:** Two opposite errors: (a) Being a fearmonger -- "99.9% of business leaders" don't have this problem; most leaders don't bring up negative stakes ENOUGH. (b) Having NO stakes at all -- "if there's nothing at stake in whether or not I buy your product, I'm not going to buy your product."

**Reversal:** For positive/aspirational brands (think Disney, luxury experiences), heavy failure messaging can feel off-brand. Use it lightly -- a gentle whisper of "what you'd miss" rather than doom language. The salt metaphor is key.

## [SB7:S7] Step 7: And Ends in Success

**Source:** Building a StoryBrand (Miller), Chapters 10-11
**Core Principle:** Never assume people understand how your brand can change their lives. Tell them. Everybody wants to be taken somewhere. Without a clear vision of success, customers will engage another brand. The success ending must be SPECIFIC and CLEAR -- not fuzzy or vague. "Kennedy would have bored the world had he cast a vision for a 'highly competitive and productive space program.' Instead: 'We're going to put a man on the moon.'"

**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: creating website imagery, writing success copy, designing product pages, crafting testimonials, defining brand promise
- AVOID WHEN: never -- every communication must include some element of the positive outcome
- COMBINES WITH: [SB7:S2] (success = resolution of all three problem levels), [SB7:S6] (success is the opposite of failure -- both create stakes)

### [SB7:S7.R] Sub-technique: Resolution of Three Problem Levels
The success section should resolve the hero's:
- External problem (the tangible outcome)
- Internal problem (how they FEEL)
- Philosophical problem (why it matters to the world)

When all three resolve simultaneously = the "climactic scene" = maximum customer satisfaction.

### [SB7:S7.P] Sub-technique: Power and Position (Status)
Heroes winning power/position = the need for status. Ways to offer status:
- **Offer access**: membership cards, loyalty programs, points (Starbucks membership)
- **Create scarcity**: limited editions, badges (Jeep "Limited" badge)
- **Offer a premium**: preferred status, Diamond Member, Emerald Club (National Car Rental)
- **Offer identity association**: brands like Mercedes/Rolex sell status alongside luxury

### [SB7:S7.U] Sub-technique: Union/Completeness (External Wholeness)
The hero is made whole by something external. Not limited to romantic union -- any "missing piece" that completes the hero. Ways to offer completeness:
- **Reduced anxiety**: product resolves frustration, brings peace and closure (cleaning products showing satisfaction)
- **Reduced workload**: tools that make customers "superhuman" (wheelbarrows, software, jackhammers)
- **More time**: products that expand time resolve the universal "I can't fit it all in" frustration

### [SB7:S7.A] Sub-technique: Self-Realization/Acceptance (Reaching Potential)
The hero reaches their potential or accepts themselves. Two forms:
- **Outward proof**: overcoming odds, achieving what seemed impossible (Rudy, Hoosiers, Legally Blonde)
- **Inward acceptance**: realizing one's worth without external proof (Bridget Jones)

Ways to offer self-realization:
- **Inspiration**: associate with athletic/intellectual accomplishment (Red Bull, Under Armour, Harvard Business Review)
- **Acceptance**: help people accept themselves (Dove "Real Beauty Sketches," American Eagle Aerie -- unretouched models)
- **Transcendence**: invite customers to participate in a larger movement (Tom's Shoes "one for one" model -- sold for $700M+; FUBU "For Us By Us")

### [SB7:S7.T] Sub-technique: Identity Transformation
The deepest motivator. "Everybody wants to change. Everybody wants to be somebody different, somebody better." Brands that participate in identity transformation create passionate evangelists.

Ask: "How does your customer want to be described by others?"

The "From -> To" framework:
- Pet food brand: From "passive dog owner" -> To "every dog's hero"
- Financial advisor: From "confused and ill-equipped" -> To "competent and smart"
- Shampoo brand: From "anxious and glum" -> To "carefree and radiant"

**Key Examples:**
- Gerber Knives "Hello Trouble": defined aspirational identity as tough, adventurous, fearless, action-oriented. Sold an identity, not just a knife. Customer paid $40 and never felt ripped off because Gerber "helped me become a better person."
- Dave Ramsey: "Where debt is dumb, cash is king, and the paid-off home mortgage has taken the place of the BMW as the status symbol of choice." Offers identity transformation + climactic scene (Debt-Free Scream) + guide affirming transformation ("You've changed. You're different now.")

### [SB7:S7.B] Sub-technique: Before and After Grid
Ryan Deiss (DigitalMarketer) tool for mapping success:

| Dimension | Before | After |
|-----------|--------|-------|
| What do they HAVE? | [before state] | [after state] |
| How do they FEEL? | [before feeling] | [after feeling] |
| What's their AVERAGE DAY like? | [before day] | [after day] |
| What's their STATUS? | [before status] | [after status] |

Fill this out for your brand to generate copy for marketing materials.

**Actionable Framework:**
1. Define specific, clear success outcomes (not vague "be your best self")
2. Show happy people using your product (images of satisfied customers)
3. Write copy that paints a picture of life after purchase
4. Address all three resolution levels (external result, internal feeling, philosophical meaning)
5. Define aspirational identity (From -> To)
6. Feature the transformation in testimonials, case studies, and website imagery
7. Use the Before/After grid to generate specific copy

**Common Mistake:** Being too vague or fuzzy about success. "We help you succeed" means nothing. Be specific: "We're going to put a man on the moon." Also: assuming customers understand how your product changes their lives. They don't. Tell them explicitly.

**Reversal:** Over-promising leads to disappointment and broken trust. Ensure the success vision is achievable and authentic. If your product delivers modest improvement, paint a picture of modest-but-meaningful improvement -- not a fantasy.

## The Three Crucial Questions (Noise Test)

At any point, a customer must be able to answer:
1. What does the hero want? (What do you offer?)
2. Who or what is opposing the hero? (How will it make my life better?)
3. What will life look like if the hero wins or loses? (What do I need to do to buy it?)

This is the "Grunt Test": Could a caveman look at your website and immediately grunt what you offer?

## The Guide Affirms the Transformation

At the end of the hero's journey, the guide must explicitly acknowledge how the hero has changed. In business: tell customers they've become different/better people through using your product. This is the final emotional payoff that creates lifelong brand loyalty.

Examples:
- Star Wars: ghost of Obi-Wan stands next to Luke during his reward ceremony
- The King's Speech: Lionel tells King George he will be a great king
- Moneyball: Peter Brand tells Billy Beane he's hit the equivalent of a home run
- Dave Ramsey: "You've changed. You're different now. There's nothing you can't accomplish."

## Why the Brain Responds to Story

**Source:** Chapter 1

The brain has two primary jobs as it relates to marketing:
1. Help the individual survive and thrive
2. Conserve calories (processing energy)

**Mistake #1:** Brands fail to focus on aspects of their offer that help people survive and thrive. All great stories are about survival -- physical, emotional, relational, or spiritual. If we position our products as anything other than an aid in helping people survive, thrive, be accepted, find love, achieve an aspirational identity, or bond with a tribe, we will fail to connect.

**Mistake #2:** Brands cause customers to burn too many calories understanding the offer. "When having to process too much seemingly random information, people begin to ignore the source of that useless information in an effort to conserve calories." There's a survival mechanism in the customer's brain designed to tune us out should we confuse them.

The brain is constantly scanning for information that helps meet primitive survival needs. When we ramble about our manufacturing plant or company history, customers don't care because that information doesn't help them eat, drink, find a mate, fall in love, build a tribe, or experience deeper meaning.

**The Noise Problem:** Business has a fierce, insidious enemy: noise. "Noise has killed more ideas, products, and services than taxes, recessions, lawsuits, climbing interest rates, and even inferior product design." What we call marketing is often clutter and confusion sprayed across websites, emails, and commercials.

**Story as Antidote:** Story is a "sense-making mechanism" that organizes information so the brain doesn't have to work to understand what's going on. "The more simple and predictable the communication, the easier it is for the brain to digest." Story is to noise what music is to random sound -- it submits information to certain rules that allow the brain to engage at a different level.

## The Hitchhiker Test

Imagine your customer is a hitchhiker. You pull over to give them a ride. The one burning question on their mind: "Where are you going?" But as they approach, you roll down the window and start talking about your mission statement, your grandfather who built the car, or your road-trip playlist of 1980s alternative music. The person doesn't care. They just want to get to San Francisco.

The goal: every potential customer knows exactly where your brand wants to take them.

## The BrandScript as Storytelling Tool

The StoryBrand BrandScript is a free digital application (mystorybrand.com) that captures your entire brand narrative on a single page. It has seven modules corresponding to the SB7 elements:

1. **Character** -- what does the customer want?
2. **Problem** -- villain + external + internal + philosophical
3. **Guide** -- empathy + authority
4. **Plan** -- process plan + agreement plan
5. **Call to Action** -- direct + transitional
6. **Failure** -- stakes, what could go wrong
7. **Success** -- resolution, what life looks like after

**Hierarchy of BrandScripts:**
- Overall brand BrandScript (primary)
- Division-level BrandScripts
- Product-level BrandScripts
- Customer-segment BrandScripts

Always start with the overall brand. Everything else is a subplot. The first project should represent the overall brand; subsequent BrandScripts can get more granular.

## StoryBrand Success Stories (from the book)

### Kyle Shultz -- Photography Course
Before StoryBrand: sold $25K in online photography courses to parents. After applying SB7 Framework: removed 90% of text from sales page, stopped using inside language like "f-stop" and "depth of field," replaced with "Take those great pictures where the background is blurry." Sent the same email to the same list 6 months later. Result: $103K in one day. Quit his day job, runs shultzphotoschool.com full-time.

### Industrial Painting Company
Website featured a fine-arts painting of their building -- looked like an Italian restaurant. Had thousands of links (FAQs, company history, nonprofits supported). Solution: wipe website clean, feature image of a guy in a white lab coat painting + text "We Paint All Kinds of S#*%" + one button: "Get a Quote."

### Fitzii (Staffing Agency)
Before: messaging was all about company strengths. After StoryBrand: revised sales process, started listening to customer needs, asking about external and internal problems. Within 7 months: 118% increase in overall revenue (more than doubled sales), 276% increase in paying customers. Last 6 months: 9% month-over-month revenue increase.

### Reed's Dairy
Before: declining email conversion rates. After StoryBrand: rewrote annual campaign email using SB7 Framework. Previous best: $3,000 in one day. After: $52,000 in one day from the same coupon campaign. Overall email conversion rates more than doubled. Extra product sales increased 12.5%.

### Marie Mae Company (Paper Products)
Before: jumbled marketing mess. After: simplified to tagline "You are changing the world from your desk. Shouldn't your office products do the same?" Revenue increased 20x. Products reached 250,000 people.

### These Numbers Have Faces (Nonprofit)
Before: playing the hero, talking about the nonprofit instead of students/donors. After: connected donors with mission narrative, told stories of heroic students. ### Mavuno (Nonprofit, Congo)
Before: complex model, unclear communications, on verge of collapse. After StoryBrand: hard pivot on communications strategy. Result: tripled annual revenue in Q4 alone. Expanded reach by 400% in eastern Congo.

### Billion-Dollar Restaurant Chain
Before: 5% growth, complacency setting in, lost their plot. After StoryBrand On-Mission implementation: went from 5% to nearly 30% growth in under 3 years -- with the same people. "For a billion-dollar company, that translated into hundreds of millions per year."

## Two Mistakes Brands Make (Mike McHargue's Analysis)

"Science Mike" McHargue (neuroscience-based marketing expert) identified two critical mistakes:

1. **Failing to focus on survival**: The subconscious is constantly sorting information. We discard millions of unnecessary facts daily. We never count chairs in a room but always know where the exits are. Why? Chairs don't help us survive; exits do. When brands talk about their backstory or internal goals, they position themselves as chairs, not exits.

2. **Causing calorie overload**: "Processing information demands that the brain burn calories. And the burning of too many calories acts against the brain's primary job: to help us survive and thrive." When you force customers onto a mental treadmill while you talk, they'll tune out fast.

The solution: make your message about something that helps the customer survive, and do so simply enough that they don't burn too many calories.

## Maslow's Hierarchy Applied to Branding

The brain prioritizes needs in this order (from Miller's discussion with Mike McHargue):

1. **Physical survival**: food, drink, shelter. In modern economy: job, dependable income
2. **Safety**: roof over head, sense of well-being and power, not being vulnerable
3. **Relationships**: sexual, romantic, friendships (tribe) for social protection
4. **Meaning**: psychological, physiological, spiritual needs that give life purpose

Marketing messages that connect to ANY of these levels will outperform those that don't. Messages that connect to NONE of these levels will be ignored regardless of how polished they are.

## The Music vs. Noise Metaphor

Technically, music and noise are similar -- both are traveling sound waves rattling eardrums. Music, however, is noise submitted to certain rules that allow the brain to engage differently. If you played someone a recording of a dump truck backing up, birds chirping, and children laughing, they wouldn't remember those sounds the next day. But a Beatles song? They'd hum it for a week.

Story is to communication what music is to sound. "A good story takes a series of random events and distills them into the essence of what really matters." In film, the final cut goes through rounds of edits, omissions, and deletions. Entire characters end up on the cutting-room floor. If a character or scene doesn't serve the plot, it has to go.

The same is true for marketing: "anything that doesn't serve the plot has to go." In every line of copy, "we're either serving the customer's story or descending into confusion; we're either making music or making noise."

## Apple: The Master StoryBrand Case Study

**Before Pixar:** In 1983, Apple launched Lisa with a nine-page ad in the New York Times spelling out technical features. Nine pages of geek talk. The computer bombed.

**After Pixar:** When Jobs returned from running Pixar (where he was surrounded by professional storytellers), Apple went from nine pages to two words on billboards: "Think Different."

**The Three Pillars Apple Understood:**
1. What customers wanted: to be seen and heard
2. The customer's challenge: people didn't recognize their hidden genius
3. A tool to express themselves: computers and smartphones

**The Mac vs. PC Campaign:** Simple, hip, fun character (Mac) next to a not-so-hip tech nerd (PC). Apple identified the internal problem: sense of intimidation most people felt about computers. They started selling a resolution to customer intimidation, not just hardware. This is one of the primary factors behind Apple's growth.

Key insight: "The story of Apple isn't about Apple; it's about you. You're the hero in the story, and they play a role more like Q in the James Bond movies."

## Steve Jobs's Transformation

The most compelling example of the SB7 Framework's power is the arc of Steve Jobs himself. Before Pixar, Jobs was a brilliant technologist who communicated through specifications and features. After years at Pixar -- surrounded by people who understood how story works -- he realized that story was everything. He brought that realization back to Apple, and the company went from near-bankruptcy to the largest technology company in the world (and one of the top ten largest companies period).

The lesson: "People don't buy the best products; they buy the products they can understand the fastest."
