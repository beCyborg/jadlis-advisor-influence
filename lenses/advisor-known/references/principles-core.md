# KNOWN: Core Principles Reference

Source: KNOWN: The Handbook for Building and Unleashing Your Personal Brand in the Digital Age, Mark W. Schaefer (2017)

This file covers the four-step KNOWN path — **Place → Space → Fuel → Audience** — plus the framing principle (Known ≠ Famous), sustainable interest, niche selection, and the eight strategies for carving out an uncontested space. Application mechanics (content rules, activation tactics, measurement, monetization) live in `application-patterns.md`.

---

## [KNW:KNOWN] Known, Not Famous — The Equity of Influence
**Source:** KNOWN, Introduction: "The Equity of Influence" & Chapter 1: "Can Anybody Become Known?"
**Core Principle:** In the digital age there is only one durable source of power and influence online: to be *known*. Traditional power symbols — job title, salary, degrees, which college you attended, executive relationships — carry almost no weight online. Being known is NOT being famous: it is having "the proper authority, reputation, and audience to realize your potential and achieve your goals, whatever they might be." Becoming known follows a repeatable, linear, four-step process (Place → Space → Fuel → Audience) that anyone can execute — but not everyone will, because it takes years of consistent work.
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: The user has a goal that "simply can't be achieved without being known" — writing a book, launching a speaking/consulting career, landing on a board, staying relevant in a field, attracting customers/donations/employers, selling digital products, running for office
- AVOID WHEN: The user wants viral fame or vanity metrics for their own sake (that is "web-famous," not known); OR it is not their *kairos* — life circumstances (see `[KNW:GRIT]`) make a multi-year commitment impossible right now
- COMBINES WITH: [KNW:SUS] (the plan behind the passion), [KNW:GRIT] (consistency is the engine that delivers on this promise)
**Key Examples:**
- Schaefer himself: went from Fortune 100 "Global Director of eBusiness" (7 patents, 2 master's degrees, 2 Chairman's Awards) to "the go-to guy for nothing" on day one as an entrepreneur — the "Veil of Silence." First two clients: a college kid's catering start-up and a local real estate agent; first speech to the Lions Club in Farragut, TN. In under five years he was consulting for Cisco, Johnson & Johnson, and Adidas.
- Cesar Paz (Brazil): "I used to be Cesar from Embraer. Now nobody knew who I was… I had to build my brand all over again!" — leaving a big company is "losing your last name."
- Alisa Meredith: "I want to be known. It's not about being famous. I'd rather just make a good living quietly. But I'm a horrible salesperson." — being known pre-populates the sales relationship.
- IBM encourages its scientists/engineers to blog and make videos so they walk into sales meetings with instant, pre-built credibility.
- Larry C. Lewis: worked a decade behind the scenes for fashion/jewelry brands and the Waldorf Astoria, then got depressed "living someone else's dream" and decided to step out and become known.
**Actionable Framework:**
1. Reframe the goal: name what the user actually wants (a board seat, clients, a book), then show that being *known* is the prerequisite.
2. Adopt the mindset shift: "Your brand isn't exactly about you. It's about how others *experience* you." Being known is "a privilege to be earned" through acceptance and amplification by an audience — no promotion, ads, or SEO trickery buys it.
3. Commit to the linear process: Place → Space → Fuel → Audience. Do not skip steps.
4. Accept the timeline: "It may take years of work to become known." This is an evolutionary process — you do not need to be an expert now.
5. Anybody can do it — including introverts (Schaefer describes himself as introverted, hating crowds, fearing public speaking) and any age (Angela Duckworth's research: people in their 60s+ are often *grittier* than younger people). If part of the path is a barricade (e.g., the disorganized-but-brilliant student), find a work-around — an editor, a VA, a coach. "Everything you need to succeed can come through focus, patience, practice, determination."
**Defense (myths the book debunks):**
- **The "Idiot's Guide to Becoming Famous"** (do something stupid → film it → get famous) produces infamy, not known status. Known ≠ web-famous.
- **"If you can dream it, you can be it"** / "follow your passion into oblivion" — "Passion without a plan is a hobby." 95%+ of start-ups fail; the #1 reason is no market need. Treat your personal brand as "the start-up of YOU."
- **"Either you have it or you don't"** — Schaefer never found a successful person who believed this. Everything needed comes through focus, patience, practice, determination (and maybe an editor/VA).
**Reversal:**
- Cultural constraint: in "tall poppy" cultures (the Dutch "we will bop you on the nose" for standing out), the barrier to being known is national/cultural, not personal shyness. Yet in a flat, global marketplace you have "almost no hope of competing unless you're known."
- Being known amplifies whatever you stand for — if you stand for nothing ("Five Great Ways to Write 15 Lists of 10" — pimping for traffic), being known creates no lasting preference and can look empty.

## [KNW:SUS] Sustainable Interest Beats Passion
**Source:** KNOWN, Chapter 2: "Step One: Your Place"
**Core Principle:** "Finding your passion is the starting line, not the finish line." A *passion* is centered on you and may be unsustainable as daily work; a *sustainable interest* is a topic you love, can be known for, that helps you reach long-term goals, AND connects to the well-being of others (purpose). Across ~100 interviews the word that came up again and again was not passion but **consistency** — "Passion… that's common. Endurance is rare." Purpose is what makes years of consistency survivable: "Instead of following your passion, sometimes your passion follows you."
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: The user has multiple passions/hobbies and can't choose; OR is about to "blast headlong" into a lifelong commitment on passion alone; OR their chosen topic is a hobby that "doesn't answer a need"
- AVOID WHEN: The user already has a validated, purpose-linked interest with a proven audience — don't re-litigate the choice, move to Space
- COMBINES WITH: [KNW:PLACE] (the exercises that surface the sustainable interest), [KNW:KNOWN] (the plan behind the passion)
**Key Examples:**
- Schaefer's own filter: boating ("Boats & Books!") and hiking ("The Middle-Aged Dude's Guide to Hiking Mostly Flat Trails in Mild Weather") are passions/hobbies — no big audience, no life-goal leverage, unsustainable daily. Teaching digital/social-media marketing is a sustainable interest: aligned with 30 years of experience, studied under Peter Drucker, "the heart of a teacher," and it provides *purpose*.
- Gary Vaynerchuk's own life disproves "the one thing in the mirror": his youthful passion was the NY Jets and sports cards, but he built his brand on wine (1,000+ wine videos) — the space with a better probability of success.
- Cal Newport (*So Good They Can't Ignore You*): "follow your passion" fails because most people can't identify a pre-existing passion, and liking a topic doesn't mean you'll like doing it as a job (amateur photographers/bakers who open businesses and hit financial ruin).
- Antonio Centeno: "My success didn't come from just having a 'passion.' I solved a burning problem that people with money are willing to pay for." His actual passion was motorcycles — he deliberately did NOT build his brand on it.
- James Altucher's interview finding: every successful person "wanted to solve a problem for the many… giving creates a bigger world."
**Actionable Framework:**
1. Test the candidate topic against four criteria: aligned with your strengths; provides purpose because it benefits others; distinctive enough to reach your goals; inexhaustibly fascinating to you.
2. Separate hobby from sustainable interest: "Sometimes, a hobby is just a hobby." Ask: is there a big enough audience to matter, and will it take me to the next level of my career?
3. Add the purpose layer: your interest must connect to the well-being of others (teaching gives Schaefer purpose; boating does not).
4. Don't obsess — "your place is evolving." You may never know your place until you're into the process; adapt, adopt, amend.
**Defense:**
- Beware the guru mantra that starts and stops at "discover your passion." Amazon has 3,000+ personal-branding books; most "start and stop right there."
- "Top-of-mind *awareness* is not the goal — top-of-mind *preference* is" (Tom Webster, Edison Research). Being known means "standing for a thing" people will rely on and count on over time.
**Reversal:**
- Over-planning becomes paralysis. Dorie Clark wrote a 700-word HBR post ("How to Reinvent Your Personal Brand") that unexpectedly caught on and led to the book *Reinventing You* — "If I had waited for the right idea, I'd probably still be waiting." The point is TRY: think it through, but don't wait for 100% certainty.

## [KNW:PLACE] Step One — Your Place
**Source:** KNOWN, Chapter 2: "Step One: Your Place" & Chapter 3: "Finding Your Sustainable Interest"
**Core Principle:** Your **Place** is a sustainable interest AND what you want to be known for — a distinct, memorable identity. "You need to be known for SOMETHING." The formula for becoming known is "the magical intersection of *place* and *space*." Chapter 3 supplies seven battle-tested exercises to surface your place; you don't need all of them — try two or three.
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: The user's positioning is generic/blurry ("I help ideas grow"), they can't say what they're known for, or they're stuck in a limiting self-definition ("I make museum displays")
- AVOID WHEN: A clear, distinctive place already exists and the bottleneck is Space, content, or audience
- COMBINES WITH: [KNW:SUS] (place must be a sustainable interest), [KNW:SPACE] (place is worthless without a large-enough space)
**Key Examples:**
- Betty Brennan (Taylor Studios): reframed from "I make museum displays" (limiting, few customers, budget cuts) to "the world's best physical storyteller" — expanded her identity into corporate "history walls," a growing part of her business.
- The malpractice-paperwork consultant whose business was killed by regulation: reframed from "insurance paperwork" to "I help doctors be doctors" — reinvented her business and headed into her most profitable year ever.
- Ann Handley (MarketingProfs): your "rich thing." Hers isn't "writing" — it's "clear, compelling communication… Regarding an audience as a privilege." "Commit to your rich thing… Rub it all over your body and wallow in it, neck deep."
**Actionable Framework:**
1. Run 2–3 of the Chapter 3 exercises (sub-techniques below).
2. Don't ask friends who see you socially or years out of date — talk to people who've worked with you extensively and recently (supervisors, employees, co-workers, customers). Have a long conversation, not a survey; listen for the keywords that define you.
3. Watch what people come to you for — the questions people ask are a clue to what you're really known for.
4. Aim for *preference and meaning* over time, not top-of-mind awareness.
**Defense:** Most personal-branding advice runs you through passion tests "and then everything stops right there." A place is not a slogan for traffic; it must be something you'll never compromise on.
**Reversal:** Don't obsess about getting your place perfect. "The ultimate result doesn't have to be perfect. It doesn't even have to be correct. But it does have to be a start." You often won't know your place until you're into the process.

### [KNW:PLACE.1] "Only I…" / Your Sentence
**Source:** KNOWN, Chapter 3
**Core Principle:** Finish the sentence "Only I…" — are you the most experienced, caring, entertaining, trustworthy; best at service or at explaining things? If you can answer it thoroughly, accurately, and confidently, "your path to becoming known will reveal itself." A variation ("What's your sentence?") distills you to one line.
**Key Examples:** Gandhi — "I am the father of passive resistance to create social change." Lincoln — "I freed the slaves." Darwin — "I developed a theory of evolution to explain biological differences." Neale Godfrey's "Only I…" — "serve as the financial voice for Baby Boomers, millennials, and their offspring."
**Actionable Framework:** If the sentence is a goal not yet achieved, ask how your sustainable interest gets you there.

### [KNW:PLACE.2] The 2×2
**Source:** KNOWN, Chapter 3
**Core Principle:** Plot the two most important factors for your target audience on a simple 2×2 and place your competitors in the boxes to visually find an uncontested opening.
**Key Examples:** Adult-education/self-improvement client. Axes: **Age** and **Cost**. The low-cost end was crowded for every age group; Lauren Elizabeth owned high-cost/youth; but "more expensive transformational opportunities for people over 40" (upper-right) was uncontested. He switched his whole focus there.
**Actionable Framework:** The easy part is naming two audience factors; the hard, important part is doing thorough research to place competitors correctly.

### [KNW:PLACE.3] The Core Values Mash-Up
**Source:** KNOWN, Chapter 3
**Core Principle:** Combine a core value/characteristic with a crowded topic to create something new and distinctive.
**Key Examples:**
- Jon Briggs — irreverence + sports = the "Un-Fan"/anti-fan (sparked by a Dolphins-logo-as-sushi t-shirt seen at a Patriots game); an ex-medical-device salesman who bought the parody rights and recruited stand-up comedians. Within two months his parodies hit 200,000+ YouTube views/week. "It's not exactly my passion… but it's something I can have fun with for years to come."
- Fanny Slater — food + humor. Left improv comedy in LA, self-taught cook, blogged food with a funny voice, won a cookbook contest on Rachael Ray's national TV show, became a Food Network co-host: "Be the Fruit Loop in the bowl of Cheerios." Also credits consistency as "the most important factor."
- Neale Godfrey (Chapter 9) — banking/money interest + the value of *empowering disadvantaged people*. First female executives at Chase, then president of the First Women's Bank; couldn't find a single book teaching children about money, so she created the category. Her "Only I…" = "serve as the financial voice for Baby Boomers, millennials, and their offspring."
- Idea generators: DIY + Frugal = "The $20 DIY Challenge"; food + family = recipes families cook together.
**Actionable Framework:** Pick your crowded topic, then mix in a value from the list (Humor, Frugal, Environment, Family, Integrity, Craftsmanship, Justice, Loyalty…) to find a fresh angle. "Don't be afraid of tackling an established topic."

### [KNW:PLACE.4] Strengths Finder — Your Superpower
**Source:** KNOWN, Chapter 3
**Core Principle:** Gallup's StrengthsFinder (14M+ takers) generates a report literally titled "What Makes You Stand-Out?" — your sustainable interest "might simply be… you."
**Key Examples:** Jane Hart became a world-recognized authority on workplace learning by naming her natural talent — a client told her: "the way you scan, curate, see patterns and then write about it is such a gift." She'd assumed everyone could do that.
**Actionable Framework:** Per Sally Hogshead (*Fascinate*): "To become more fascinating, you don't have to change who you are. You have to become more of who you are, at your best."

### [KNW:PLACE.5] The Beautiful Questions & The 35 Headlines
**Source:** KNOWN, Chapter 3
**Core Principle:** Warren Berger's "beautiful questions" surface a sustainable interest; the "35 headlines" list stress-tests whether it can be sustained.
**Key Examples of the questions:** "What is your tennis ball?" (Drew Houston/Dropbox MIT speech — the thing that pulls you). "What am I doing when I feel most alive?" (Martin Lindstrom: your "one room" at home and your Facebook cover photo reveal what you value). "What do you believe that almost nobody agrees with?" (Peter Thiel — go "through the open door no one is seeing"). "What are you willing to try now?" (experiment via moonlighting — Schaefer tested consulting gigs and found he *hated* some, which saved him).
**Actionable Framework (35 Headlines):** List the first 35 topics you'd cover about your idea — just headlines. First 10–20 flow easily; the last 10–15 take deep thought. Two insights: (1) if you struggle to reach 35, your interest may not be sustainable over months/years; (2) the last 10 topics often refine or refocus the interest. (Pete Matthew's rule of thumb: if you can't think of 50 subject lines, don't start.)
**Also (Exercise 6 — Visualize Your Future):** imagine yourself two years out being interviewed on a popular podcast, delivering a closing keynote, and being urged by friends to write a book — then ask what questions you're asked, what topic you speak on, and what skill you'd share. "Being known for something in the future doesn't require you to be an expert now. You have two years to get there."

## [KNW:SPACE] Step Two — Your Space (The Uncontested Niche)
**Source:** KNOWN, Chapter 4: "Step Two: Determining Your Space"
**Core Principle:** Your **Space** is "an uncontested or under-occupied niche with enough people to matter." A sustainable interest is useless if presented in a channel so crowded you're never discovered. "Failing to find a meaningful, uncontested space is the primary reason people fail in their effort to become known." Willie "Wee Willie" Keeler's hitting rule is the strategy: **"Hit 'em where they ain't."**
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: The user has a place but is invisible; OR is about to create content without checking saturation; OR their space "is entirely flooded with competition who have been doing this far longer, and far better"
- AVOID WHEN: The niche is already validated as open and the bottleneck is content or audience
- COMBINES WITH: [KNW:PLACE] (place × space intersection), [KNW:8S] (the eight strategies for carving space when you're not first)
**Key Examples:**
- Dr. Jamie Goode: started Wine Anorak, blogged in 2001 — "the second person in the world with a wine blog." Between 2001–2004 wine blogs went from 1 to 700+; if he'd waited a year he'd have been "swept away by the blogging typhoon." First-mover advantage is real but vulnerable — consistency defends it.
- Greg Kinman ("Hickok45"): retired middle-school English teacher, Pleasant View TN (pop. 4,149). He shoots guns on YouTube — 2M+ subscribers, one video ~15M views — via place + open space + three videos/week for years.
- Kevin Kelly (*The Inevitable*): "There has never been a better day in the whole history of the world to invent something… You are not late."
- Antonio Centeno's five foundational elements (Introduction) show place × space in action: the right *place* (fashion advice = sustainable interest), the right *space* (fashion-tip *videos* = wide-open niche), *consistent content* (200 videos in 200 days, then 1,000 in 1,000), a *meaningful audience* (big enough to monetize), and *patience* (four years before a significant payoff — "singles and doubles," not home runs).
**Actionable Framework (assessing your niche):**
1. Google your candidate keywords. Rough saturation read: **< 500,000 results** = extremely good, you could be a true pioneer; **500K–1M** = possible with patient work; **> 1M** = saturated, tweak the angle or find a new one.
2. Check direction of interest (Google Trends). Rising interest + thin content = a wide-open runway.  <!-- privacy-ok: термин из книжного конспекта, не финансы владельца -->
3. "Owns" test: millions of *articles* on a topic doesn't mean anyone *owns* it — you may still become the person known for it.
**Defense:** The three saturation questions — Is the niche big enough to matter? Is it already oversaturated? Are there people who will be attracted to it? Don't confuse "lots of content exists" with "the space is taken."
**Reversal:** A niche can be *too* small — a place with no actionable audience means "what in the world are you doing?" Balance uncontested against big-enough-to-matter.

### [KNW:SPACE.1] First-Mover Advantage
**Source:** KNOWN, Chapter 4
**Core Principle:** "Often, being first is more important than being the best." Establishing an early foothold lets you grow without competition — but the moment you stop being timely and relevant, you endanger the status. Consistency is the defense.
**Key Examples:** Dr. Jamie Goode's early arrival (2001) let him "grow without a lot of competition"; he now makes his living from the wine blog. "If you aren't excited about something, then why would your readers be excited?"

### [KNW:SPACE.2] The Niche Saturation Test (Google-count & KOI)
**Source:** KNOWN, Chapter 4 & Appendix II ("Determining content density in regions of the world")
**Core Principle:** Quantify competition before you commit. Basic method = raw Google result counts (above). For regional/refined analysis, use Roy Ishak's Keyword Optimization Index (KOI).
**Key Examples:** "Babymoon" = 316,000 results + rising Trends → wide-open ("king and queen of the babymoon world"). "Succession planning" = 9M+ results, but no single person owns it → still winnable.
**Actionable Framework (KOI):** (1) Get search volume via Google AdWords Keyword Planner; (2) get competition via `allintitle:keyword`; (3) `search_volume / allintitle_results × search_volume` — higher number = better chance to rank. Narrow by country by selecting the country in the planner and using the national Google (google.nl, google.fr…) for the `allintitle` count.

## [KNW:8S] Eight Space Strategies (When You're Not First)
**Source:** KNOWN, Chapter 5: "Eight Space Strategies"
**Core Principle:** Most people aren't lucky enough to be first (like Dr. Goode). You can still carve a workable space eight ways: "Perhaps you can be known for how and where you tell your story." Pick the strategy that fits your place, personality, and competitive landscape.
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: The user's niche is crowded and they need a differentiated angle rather than abandoning the topic
- AVOID WHEN: They already hold an uncontested first-mover position — just defend it with consistency
- COMBINES WITH: [KNW:SPACE] (this is the "how" when first-mover isn't available), [KNW:FUEL]/[KNW:RITE] (strategies 3 & 4 are content-type/form decisions)
**Actionable Framework:** Do a competitive analysis first — who are the leaders in your field and where do they publish? Then choose one (or a combination) of the eight sub-strategies below.

### [KNW:8S.1] Develop a Unique Tone or Point of View
**Source:** KNOWN, Chapter 5
**Core Principle:** Stand out by having a recognizable, distinctive voice — "having the courage to let your freak flag fly." Your tone must be a natural extension of your personality, or it's exhausting to sustain. "James Altucher writes a quirky blog because he's quirky."
**Key Examples:**
- James Altucher — the "world's most unlikely self-help guru," raw and confessional in the crowded self-help field. Sample headlines: "Teach your kid how to kill people," "Ten things the aliens forgot to tell us." A young startup millionaire who lost it all twice; began "Altucher Confidential" chronicling his failures. "I've made every mistake in the book: Here's what they are." "The number one search phrase on Google that takes people to my blog is 'I want to die.'" His self-published *Choose Yourself* sold 500,000+ copies (WSJ bestseller). "Advice is autobiography."
- Isadora Becker — cooks recipes from famous movie/TV food scenes dressed as the characters: Marge Simpson (yellow paint + wig) making Homer's donuts; the *Pulp Fiction* $5 milkshake; Walter White making sky-blue candy. Built her audience four years, then a cookbook and a TV show; 180,000 subscribers.

### [KNW:8S.2] Move to a New Social Platform Within Your Niche
**Source:** KNOWN, Chapter 5
**Core Principle:** Build an audience on a platform your competitors underserve. "Do you KNOW where your audience is today? Do your competitors?"
**Key Examples:** Suzy Trotta — Knoxville realtor in a county with 6,000+ agents. While rivals spam Facebook/Twitter with open-house posts, she posts on Instagram the absurd stuff sellers leave behind (plastic donkeys, ceramic cat planters) and pure-joy closing photos. A client bonded with her over cat pictures and gave her three transactions. Her last nine hires all found her online.

### [KNW:8S.3] Dominate a Content Type (Hygiene / Hub / Hero)
**Source:** KNOWN, Chapter 5
**Core Principle:** YouTube's model of three content types. **Hygiene** = daily how-to/FAQ content (best for organic search, most common). **Hub** = addictive, binge-able series that keeps people on your site. **Hero** = bold, viral, expensive tentpole content. 90%+ of niches are dominated by hygiene content — so hub or hero is often the opening.
**Key Examples:** Rand Fishkin (Moz) turned the "gray world of SEO" into "Whiteboard Friday" hub videos — two years before real traction, three years before they matched his text blog; Moz now gets 3M+ monthly visits.

### [KNW:8S.4] Pioneer a New Content Form
**Source:** KNOWN, Chapter 5 (see also the PewDiePie story, Chapter 9)
**Core Principle:** Revive or invent a content form few in your niche use — "the power of combinatory skills." Putting two types of expertise together gives you a point of differentiation.
**Key Examples:**
- Tom Fishburne ("The Marketoonist") — a working marketer who draws marketing cartoons. "ONLY I would be foolish enough to take out business school loans to become a cartoonist!" Took 10 years from his first HBS-newspaper cartoon to leaving his day job (General Mills, Nestlé). Editorial cartoonists at US dailies fell to <40 (from ~200 in the early 1990s, ~2,000 at the start of the 20th century) — an old form perfectly suited to the digital age. "Cartoons are the original snack-able content… a participatory medium."
- Felix Kjellberg ("PewDiePie," Chapter 9) — dropped out of college, sold hot dogs on a street corner to fund his gaming videos, was locked out of his account and restarted from scratch. He essentially pioneered a new content form: "commentary entertainment" — reacting on-camera to a game he plays. First person on YouTube to pass 10 billion views; nearly 50M subscribers; eight-figure income. "It's so personal and human that it's almost like being on a Skype call with the guy."

### [KNW:8S.5] Differentiate Through Frequency
**Source:** KNOWN, Chapter 5 (see also Pete Matthew, Chapter 9)
**Core Principle:** Own an open cadence — either an underserved high frequency or a deliberately rare one.
**Key Examples:**
- John Lee Dumas ("Entrepreneur On Fire") — ex-Army (Iraq), bounced through law school, finance, a startup, and real estate before spotting that no podcast published *daily* interviews. His daily show ("Fire Nation") earns $1M+/year: "Valuable Content, Consistent Content, Free Content." Featured in *Forbes* within two years.
- Avinash Kaushik (Google) — posts on digital analytics only once a month, but each is a ~10,000-word epic; the *infrequency* itself signals "pay attention."
- Pete Matthew (financial planner, Penzance, England, Chapter 9) — "I didn't want my legacy to be just helping rich people get richer. I wanted to make a dent in the universe." Found a vacuum: nobody in the financial space made consistent content. Shot 3 videos/week (~300 in 2.5 years) — it took 18 months for the first prospective client to approach him. Then the *Meaningful Money* podcast (2012); "nothing really happened until I became more consistent… When I published weekly, things started to take off." Now ~1,000 downloads/day globally.

### [KNW:8S.6] Appeal to a New Demographic or Geographic Niche
**Source:** KNOWN, Chapter 5
**Core Principle:** Think local/regional/underrepresented — "You don't necessarily have to be the best in the world, just better or more knowledgeable than those around you."
**Key Examples:**
- Juan Pablo Zurita ("JuanPa") — an 18-year-old Mexican who first mimicked US YouTubers in English, then found far less competition (and far more traction) in Spanish-language comedy videos; became an international YouTube star.
- Lindsey Kirchoff — a 20-year-old Tufts student who, instead of blasting résumés, blogged "How to Market to Me" about marketing to millennials on LinkedIn. The demographic niche made her known: keynote at an automotive conference, quoted in the *LA Times*, recruited to a Campbell's Soup millennial think tank.
- Suzy Trotta likewise needs only her local Knoxville sales territory — "If you can reach your goals by becoming known on a local level, focus on that as a point of differentiation."

### [KNW:8S.7] Leverage Relationships With Influencers
**Source:** KNOWN, Chapter 5
**Core Principle:** "When you don't have time to build your own audience, borrow someone else's." Give value to influencers first — the *reciprocity* principle (sociologist Alvin Gouldner: no human society lacks this rule) makes them promote you.
**Key Examples:** Shawn Van Dyke — construction consultant (wife with MS, five kids). Validated his idea via the "Skilled Trade Sound Business Project," gave away a book, built on Instagram, then offered *free* consulting to a well-known Boston builder who reposted him as "his coach" — "My social media audience exploded all at once." He scaled it via a private Facebook group of influencers and landed his first national speaking invitation.

### [KNW:8S.8] Use Curation as a Niche
**Source:** KNOWN, Chapter 5
**Core Principle:** Summarize the best of a topic for a time-starved audience. "Typically, every topic needs only one curator, and if you're first on the scene, you'll own the niche." Works when you lack time/talent to create original content, and in regulated industries (pharma, banking, insurance) where original content is risky.
**Key Examples:** Christopher Korody — became a drone-industry authority (and built a consulting business) by curating drone news daily, without a single original piece. Scott Monty — "The Full Monty" weekly newsletter with witty commentary. Best practices: vary sources, add your own voice, commit to a schedule, use tools (BuzzSumo, Feedly).

## [KNW:FUEL] Step Three — Your Fuel (Rich Content)
**Source:** KNOWN, Chapter 6: "Step Three: Finding Your Fuel"
**Core Principle:** "Content is the fuel that makes all of this happen… the rungs of the ladder are content." To be *discoverable* (found by search, reporters, prospects) you need **rich content** with the depth and breadth to rank — not fleeting social posts. Choose ONE of four rich-content types and stick with it for at least a year: **written (blog), audio (podcast), video series, or visual content.** Google rewards consistent, quality content over time — which is why becoming known takes months or years.
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: The user has place + space but no discoverable content engine, or is spreading thin across every platform at once
- AVOID WHEN: They're a politician/celebrity/Kardashian-tier figure who gets a "free pass"; otherwise everyone must create
- COMBINES WITH: [KNW:RITE] (the rules and RITE test for the content itself), [KNW:8S.3]/[KNW:8S.4] (content-type and content-form as space strategies), [KNW:ACT] (content is what activates the audience)
**Key Examples:**
- The influence-marketing story: a single 2011 blog post (shared ~2,000 times) signaled Google that it was special in an uncontested niche → a NYT reporter found it via search → four quotes in the Sunday NYT → syndication (*Daily Mail* etc.) → the book *Return On Influence*, global speaking, and a Japan consulting assignment. All from one *rich* post — Facebook/Twitter/LinkedIn posts would never have been found by that reporter.
- Podcast audiences (Edison Research): more loyal, better educated, wealthier than other online audiences.
- Amazon is the #3 search engine (after Google and YouTube) — another reason rich, discoverable content matters.
- Jeff Bullas — broke, unemployed, mid-divorce, $50,000 in debt; read "even if you only have an inkling of what you want to do, just start," began a blog though "I was terrible!" Now one of the top marketing blogs on the web. "Blogging literally changed my life."

**The four types of rich content (choose ONE to build for ≥1 year):**
- **Written (blog):** easy/low-cost to start, time-consuming to maintain; primary driver of search ranking and thought-leadership/community.
- **Audio (podcast):** an internet radio show; loyal, educated, wealthy listeners; moderate-to-high cost depending on frequency.
- **Video series:** best to entertain/demonstrate/teach; high engagement, popular with younger audiences; cost varies from live-streaming to complex productions.
- **Visual (infographics, illustrations, photos):** ideal for Facebook/Instagram/Pinterest; high social-sharing; reasonable cost.
**Actionable Framework (choosing your type):**
1. **Find the open space** — "hit 'em where they ain't"; look for an under-served content type in your niche.
2. **Figure out what you love** — you may spend years creating; if you don't love it, you'll quit.
3. **Match content to personality** — introvert-leaning forms (blogging, books, webinars, visual/Pinterest/Instagram, interview podcasts/videos) vs extrovert-leaning (Snapchat, live-streaming, solo videos/podcasts, Twitter chats); and "quiet" vs high-engagement distribution platforms.
4. **Pick one, do it excellently, build there ≥1 year.** "Resist the temptation to do everything and be everywhere. Focus, focus, focus."
5. **Find the time** — everyone has the same hours; the issue is tenacity, not time. Schaefer's 20-tip essence: block undistracted hours like a meeting; add an insight to others' posts (attribute + add 2 ideas); "imperfect is perfect — push publish"; don't multitask (knowledge workers lose ~28% of time to interruptions); keep a content calendar; repurpose one core idea into many formats; narrate/transcribe (you speak ~3× faster than you type); outsource editing/producing; learn to say no.
**Defense:** The three SEO signals that made the influence post work — **age/size of site** (four years of consistent content before Google noticed), **backlinks** (quality/diversity of incoming links), **dwell time** (people stayed and read). "The very best thing you can do for SEO is to create helpful, relevant, and timely content." Don't obsess about SEO tricks.
**Reversal:** "I can't write" / "I don't have time" are surmountable — if you can't write, make videos; if not videos, podcast. And it's fine to get help (writing coach, editor, ghost/transcriber) *as long as* the ideas are yours, you're accountable for every word, and you never mislead people. Jeff Bullas was "terrible" at first and built a top marketing blog.

## [KNW:AUD] Step Four — Creating an Actionable Audience
**Source:** KNOWN, Chapter 7: "Step Four: Creating an Actionable Audience"
**Core Principle:** "If content is your fuel, the audience is your fire." "The value of your content is zero unless somebody views it." Your network is your net worth — but two myths must die first: (1) you do NOT need a million fans, and (2) **audience size does not equal power (activation).** The goal is an *actionable* audience — people with whom you've built a genuine emotional connection over time, who will actually buy, hire, donate, or advocate.
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: The user has content but no audience, or is chasing follower counts / assuming reach = results
- AVOID WHEN: They already have a right-sized, engaged, actionable audience and the bottleneck is monetization or amplification
- COMBINES WITH: [KNW:ACT] (the passive/active mechanics of building it), [KNW:ALPHA] (the elite advocate segment), [KNW:GRIT] (audience is built "one person, one connection at a time," over years)
**Key Examples:**
- **The big-enough niche — Dr. Alice Ackerman:** a pediatrics chair blogging to raise childhood-vaccination rates. For over a year she averaged fewer than five readers a day (<25 subscribers) — then one reader inoculated her children and pledged to spread the word. "Yes, I only had five readers a day, but they were the right five readers." Becoming known ≠ being famous; sometimes one connection is enough.
- **Audience ≠ activation — Alyssa Milano:** tweeted an Amazon link for the book *Connected* (Christakis & Fowler) to 3M followers. Books sold: **none** — sales actually declined. "These Twitter links are weak." Size didn't create a "force for anything" because there was no emotional connection to the academic book.
- Estimated audiences of people in the book vary wildly (Antonio Centeno ~750K YouTube subs; Isadora Becker ~180K; Tom Fishburne tens of thousands of newsletter subs; Suzy Trotta ~2,000 Twitter followers mostly local; Lindsey Kirchoff ~500 LinkedIn connections; Shawn Van Dyke dozens in a private Facebook group; Dr. Ackerman <25 subscribers) — "there's no universal answer to 'how big does my audience need to be?'"
- **Actionable-audience built for action — Jennifer James (Social Good Moms, Chapter 9):** already ran the world's largest community of mom bloggers (~18,000). After a 2012 Kenya trip she asked her community to found "Social Good Moms" — 400 women signed up in one day; now 3,000+ who spread the word on global health/development. "Action begins with awareness and education." Her differentiator was sustaining the community with consistent connection over years where others failed.
**Actionable Framework:**
1. Define the *right* audience for YOUR goal, not the biggest one. "Maybe all you need is one follower if it's a person who will change the course of your life."
2. Understand that a "like" is a handshake — raw potential, a cracked-open door, not a customer.
3. Build activation the only way it works: "establish a legitimate emotional connection with people over time." (Passive and active mechanics → `[KNW:ACT]`.)
**Defense:** "Social networks are effective at increasing participation by lessening the level of motivation that participation requires" — a click costs nothing; buying, showing up, or donating costs investment. Don't mistake cheap participation for influence.
**Reversal:** Chasing a huge audience can dilute the connection that made you stand out. Ana Silva O'Reilly deliberately doesn't want her blog "to get much bigger… I feel I would lose that connection." Bigger is not always the goal.
