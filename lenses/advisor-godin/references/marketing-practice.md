## 1. [TIM:FN] The Funnel

**Source:** This Is Marketing (Godin), Chapter 19
**Core Principle:** Visualize a funnel with leaks and holes. At the top, you pour attention. At the bottom, committed loyal customers come out. Between top and bottom, most people leak out. The status of trust changes at every step. You can fix your funnel, but don't expect a magical perpetual-motion machine — "most brands that matter, and most organizations that thrive, are primed by advertising but built by good marketing."
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: You're buying ads; you need to understand why traffic doesn't convert; you want to optimize your customer journey
- AVOID WHEN: You haven't built something worth buying yet — fix the product first, not the funnel
- COMBINES WITH: `[TIM:TT]` (trust moves people through the funnel), `[TIM:PM]` (permission feeds the funnel), `[TIM:TN]` (tension at each step creates forward motion), `[TIM:NE]` (the bottom of the funnel feeds the top)

**Key Examples:**
- **Casey Neistat funnel math:** 1,000,000 YouTube views → 18,000 Twitch clicks → 1,000 comments → 5 signups. "That's what a funnel looks like." (Ch. 19)
- **Supermarket lifetime value:** Single trip profit = $1-2. But a loyal customer visiting twice/week for 5 years = $50,000 in sales, $1,000 profit. Worth apologizing over a $4 melon. (Ch. 19)
- **Stamps analogy:** If sending 1,000 letters costs $500 and lifetime customer value is $700 — buy as many stamps as you can. If LTV is $400 — you need a better letter or a better business. (Ch. 19)

**Actionable Framework:**
1. **Fix your funnel in 6 ways:** (a) Attract the right people, (b) Align your promise with your destination, (c) Remove unnecessary steps, (d) Support and reinforce dreams at each step, (e) Use tension for forward motion, (f) Give successful customers a megaphone
2. **The key equation:** Lifetime value of a customer vs. cost of acquiring them through the funnel
3. "If you can't see the funnel, don't buy the ads."
4. "If the funnel costs too much, don't buy the ads. Fix the funnel first."
5. Every click between first and last makes the funnel more expensive, but removing too many clicks means no one trusts you enough to buy

**Reversal:** "The truth is that most brands that matter are primed by advertising but built by good marketing. They grow because users evangelize." Don't optimize the funnel while ignoring the product. A perpetual-motion funnel of profit is a fantasy.

### [TIM:FN.1] Funnel Math

**Source:** This Is Marketing (Godin), Chapter 19
**Core Principle:** Calculate the lifetime value (LTV) of a customer. Then compare it to the cost of acquisition through the funnel. If LTV > acquisition cost, scale aggressively. If not, fix the funnel or the business. "I'm guessing that fewer than 5% of all advertisers measured their results in the old days. Today the number is closer to 60%. What's missing is a thoughtful analysis of what those numbers mean."
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: Deciding whether to buy ads; evaluating channel efficiency; prioritizing marketing spend
- AVOID WHEN: You don't know your LTV — calculate it first
- COMBINES WITH: `[TIM:FN]` (parent), `[TIM:DM]` (direct marketing requires this math)

**Key Examples:**
- **Slack early customers:** An early customer's LTV was potentially $50,000+ when counting impact on coworkers, competitive lockout, and equity value. "The first thousand customers, if they're the right people, are practically priceless." (Ch. 19)
- **L.L. Bean, Lands' End, Victoria's Secret:** "That simple analysis is why you've heard of them. They bought a lot of stamps." (Ch. 19)

**Actionable Framework:**
1. Calculate LTV: single purchase profit + repeat purchases + referral value + competitive lockout value
2. Calculate cost per acquisition: ad spend / conversions at each funnel step
3. If LTV > CPA: scale. If LTV < CPA: fix before spending more
4. Don't just measure — analyze what the numbers mean
5. "You'll serve many people. You'll profit from a few. The whales pay for the minnows."

**Reversal:** "Very few organizations do this math carefully. They're spending and praying, hoping it all comes out in the wash."

### [TIM:FN.2] Long Tail and Short Head

**Source:** This Is Marketing (Godin), Chapter 19
**Core Principle:** Half of Amazon's sales are books NOT in the top 5,000. The long tail is vast but individual positions on it are untenable. The solution: "by dividing the market into many curves, we end up with many short heads and many long tails." Create the hit that unites a specific micro-market. Be the short head of YOUR long tail.
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: You're struggling in a huge marketplace; your product is invisible among millions; you need to find your position
- AVOID WHEN: You're already the short head of your niche
- COMBINES WITH: `[TIM:SVA]` (find your specific market), `[TIM:PLU]` (the hit connects "people like us"), `[TIM:NE]` (hits grow through network effects)

**Key Examples:**
- **April Fools' Passover Birthday Easter shirt:** A long-tail T-shirt company making tens of thousands of designs — individually each sells nearly zero, but taken together, thousands per month. (Ch. 19)
- **Rocky Horror Picture Show:** Short head of its specific long tail — "the hit that unites us, the one that makes it clear that you are people like us." (Ch. 19)
- **DeWalt 20V Max XR brushless hammer drill:** Short head of its specific niche. (Ch. 19)

**Actionable Framework:**
1. You can't live on the generic long tail — "The internet can live on that, but you can't"
2. Divide the market into specific curves
3. Create the definitive contribution to your specific field
4. Connect the market so they see you belong in the short head
5. "Becoming an outlier isn't a strategy. It's a wish."

**Reversal:** Being on the long tail is "the false promise of the internet." Don't mistake having a listing for having a business.

## 2. [TIM:DM] Direct Marketing vs. Brand Marketing

**Source:** This Is Marketing (Godin), Chapter 15
**Core Principle:** Direct marketing is action oriented and measured. Brand marketing is culturally oriented and can't be measured. Confusing the two is the most expensive mistake in marketing. "If you're buying direct marketing ads, measure everything. If you're buying brand marketing ads, be patient. Refuse to measure."
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: Deciding how to spend marketing budget; evaluating whether an ad campaign "worked"; choosing between measurable performance and cultural investment
- AVOID WHEN: You confuse the two — never measure brand marketing like direct marketing, never run direct marketing without measurement
- COMBINES WITH: `[TIM:FN]` (direct marketing feeds the funnel), `[TIM:TT]` (brand marketing builds trust), `[TIM:PM]` (both aim for permission)

**Key Examples:**
- **Facebook/Google ads:** Action-oriented. $100 in ads should generate $125+ in profit. Keep buying until they stop paying for themselves. This is direct marketing. (Ch. 15)
- **Billboard by the highway:** Hoping people remember your funeral parlor when someone dies. Can't be measured. This is brand marketing. (Ch. 15)
- **Procter & Gamble:** Billions on TV brand advertising for Tide and Crest. "When TV brand ads are replaced with digital direct ads, their business model falls apart." (Ch. 15)
- **Pizza place and Yellow Pages:** Yellow Pages was measurable direct marketing with a special phone number. Moving to Yelp is harder — "no control, no proven history." (Ch. 15)

**Actionable Framework:**

**Direct Marketing:**
1. The ad exists to get a click
2. The click exists to make a sale or earn permission
3. The sale leads to another sale or word of mouth
4. Permission leads to education and a sale
5. "Assign values to each step. If you can't, don't run any direct-response ads until you can."

**Brand Marketing:**
1. Everything you do is brand marketing — phone manner, packaging, location, packing peanuts
2. Be consistent. Be patient. Be specific.
3. "You definitely, certainly, and surely don't have enough time and money to build a brand for everyone"
4. "Every slice of every interaction ought to reflect the whole"
5. Overdo it for your specific audience — don't spread thin for everyone

**Reversal:** "The two paragraphs about measuring direct / refusing to measure brand ought to have paid for the time and money you've spent on this book." Most organizations confuse the two and waste fortunes.

### [TIM:DM.1] Simple Guide to Online Direct Marketing

**Source:** This Is Marketing (Godin), Chapter 15
**Core Principle:** Ad → Click → Sale (or Permission) → Repeat Sale (or Education → Sale). That's it. Every step has a cost and a yield. "Will some people see your ads without taking action? Definitely. That's a side effect, not the goal."
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: Running any online paid campaign; evaluating Google/Facebook ad spend
- AVOID WHEN: You can't measure the funnel — "If you can't measure it, it doesn't count"
- COMBINES WITH: `[TIM:FN]` (funnel math), `[TIM:PM]` (permission as intermediate goal)

**Actionable Framework:**
1. Measure cost per click
2. Measure conversion from click to sale (or permission)
3. Measure lifetime value from sale
4. If LTV > total cost per acquisition: scale
5. Focus on improving yield at each step, not just lowering click costs
6. "If you lower click costs by making ridiculous promises, it'll backfire — trust will evaporate"

**Reversal:** Don't trick people into clicks. Cheap clicks from misleading ads destroy funnel efficiency.

### [TIM:DM.2] Advertising is Optional

**Source:** This Is Marketing (Godin), Chapter 15
**Core Principle:** Advertising is bought attention — unearned media. "You didn't pay the recipient to run that ad, but you want the recipient to pay you with their attention. So you're ignored." The internet is the most ignored advertising medium ever created. "It's not unusual to run an ad in front of a hundred thousand people and get not a single click."
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: You're assuming you need ads to grow; you're spending on ads before building organic word-of-mouth
- AVOID WHEN: You've proven funnel economics and want to scale — then ads make sense
- COMBINES WITH: `[TIM:PM]` (earn attention instead of buying it), `[TIM:TT]` (trust beats interruption)

**Actionable Framework:**
1. "Don't get distracted by the possibility of free attention, a spotlight that magically makes you famous"
2. Ads work when they create tension — "The tension of not knowing and needing to know more"
3. Most TV advertising is just semiotic noise — reassuring viewers that you can afford to be on TV
4. Before buying ads: have you earned organic traction? If not, fix that first.

**Reversal:** "It's not that advertising can't work. It's simply that it's not the right answer for everyone, at least in this moment."

## 3. [TIM:PR] Pricing (as Signal)

**Source:** This Is Marketing (Godin), Chapter 16
**Core Principle:** Price is a story. "Marketing changes your pricing. Pricing changes your marketing." Price should be aligned with the extremes you claimed in your positioning. Price is more than a number — it's the engine for growth because it creates margin, and margin funds marketing. "Low price is the last refuge of a marketer who has run out of generous ideas."
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: Setting prices; defending premium pricing; tempted to race to the bottom; considering free
- AVOID WHEN: You think price is just about extracting money — it's about signaling position and funding change
- COMBINES WITH: `[TIM:ST]` (price signals status), `[TIM:SM]` (price is a symbol), `[TIM:SVA]` (price selects your audience), `[TIM:TT]` (higher prices can BUILD trust)

**Key Examples:**
- **The baker at three price points:** $2.00/loaf = $0.05 profit. $2.50 = $0.55 profit (11x more). $3.00 = $1.05 profit (21x the $2 loaf). The $2 baker needs to sell 21 loaves for every 1 the $3 baker sells. (Ch. 16)
- **Wine on the menu:** "Are you the kind of person who buys the cheapest wine? The most expensive?" — neither question mentions taste or value. Just price. (Ch. 16)
- **Porsche Cayenne:** "No conceivable utility proportionate to its expense. It's merely a signal." (Ch. 16)
- **USHG (Union Square Hospitality Group) no-tipping:** Raised prices 20%, eliminated tipping. Gave diners a story to tell — about fairness, dignity, and choosing a restaurant that aligns with their worldview. (Ch. 16)
- **Tesla pricing:** Fixed price — no haggling. Luxury car buyers "breathed a sigh of relief." (Ch. 16)

**Actionable Framework:**
1. "Better to apologize for the price once than to have to excuse a hundred small slights again and again"
2. Price = signal. Higher price = more margin = better experience = more referrals = more growth
3. "'Cheap' is another way to say 'scared'" — unless you've found an extraordinary new delivery method
4. "When you're the cheapest, you're not promising change. You're promising the same, but cheaper."
5. Calculate: what would change if you charged 10x more? "More of the same" is the wrong answer.

**Reversal:** Price too low destroys trust. "Lowering your price doesn't make you more trusted. It does the opposite." Cognitive dissonance means people invent trust around high-cost commitments.

### [TIM:PR.1] Free as a Strategy

**Source:** This Is Marketing (Godin), Chapter 16
**Core Principle:** Free is not just a penny less — it's "an entirely different category of transaction" that scales to infinity. A free idea spreads far faster than a paid one. But you can't make a living giving everything away. The solution: "Free ideas that spread + Expensive expressions of those ideas that are worth paying for."
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: You want maximum spread of ideas; building permission; content marketing strategy
- AVOID WHEN: Everything is free with no paid tier; you're giving away the souvenirs, not the ideas
- COMBINES WITH: `[TIM:PM]` (free ideas earn permission), `[TIM:TT]` (free builds trust for the paid offering)

**Key Examples:**
- **Chef giving away recipes:** Recipes are free. Pasta on china at the restaurant costs $24. "The china and the ticket are souvenirs of ideas, and souvenirs are supposed to be expensive." (Ch. 16)
- **Radio songs + concert tickets:** Song on radio = free. Concert ticket = $84. Artist is compensated. (Ch. 16)
- **Facebook at $3/month:** Would have attracted fewer than a million users. (Ch. 16)

**Actionable Framework:**
1. Give away ideas (digital, scalable, shareable)
2. Charge for expressions/experiences (physical, scarce, personal)
3. "Each free interaction builds awareness, permission, and trust — a platform to sell the thing worth paying for"
4. Free creates enrollment — paid creates commitment

**Reversal:** Making everything free means "engaging in a transaction is fundamentally different than encountering an apparently worthless object." Without cash flow, you can't invest in product, team, or marketing.

### [TIM:PR.2] Trust and Expense Paradox

**Source:** This Is Marketing (Godin), Chapter 16
**Core Principle:** Rationally, we should want more trust before risky/expensive transactions. But often the opposite is true — the fact that a transaction is risky causes cognitive dissonance: "I'm a smart person, so I must trust this person." High cost creates trust, not the other way around.
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: Your price feels "too high" and you're tempted to lower it; you need to understand why premium pricing works psychologically
- AVOID WHEN: You're using this to justify overpricing without delivering value
- COMBINES WITH: `[TIM:PR]` (parent), `[TIM:TT]` (price and trust are linked), `[TIM:ST]` (expensive = high status)

**Key Examples:**
- **Boot camp:** High cost of participation (blood, sweat, tears) causes alignment with the group. (Ch. 16)
- **Outward Bound:** "That's why people change at Outward Bound." The investment creates the trust, not the reverse. (Ch. 16)
- **High-end restaurants/hotels:** Can survive bad reviews because cognitive dissonance protects them — customers invested too much to admit they were wrong. (Ch. 16)

**Actionable Framework:**
1. "Lowering your price doesn't make you more trusted. It does the opposite."
2. When people invest heavily (cash/reputation/effort), they make up stories to justify their commitment
3. These stories carry trust — and become self-reinforcing
4. Be generous with change and brave with your business — charge enough to deliver amazingly

**Reversal:** Every con man knows this. The ethical version: charge enough to deliver transformation, not just a product.

## 4. [TIM:NE] Network Effects

**Source:** This Is Marketing (Godin), Chapters 8, 19
**Core Principle:** The bridge across the chasm lies in network effects. Products that work better when more people use them have a built-in engine for growth. "Connected tribes are more powerful than disconnected ones. Individuals who get in early have an incentive to bring others along, and so they do." Metcalfe's Law: the value of a network increases exponentially with each new member.
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: Designing products or services; thinking about growth strategy; trying to cross from early adopters to mass market
- AVOID WHEN: Your product genuinely doesn't benefit from more users — then focus on other growth levers
- COMBINES WITH: `[TIM:SVA]` (start with the smallest viable network), `[TIM:TN]` (network effects create "left behind" tension), `[TIM:PLU]` (network effects reinforce identity), `[TIM:FN]` (network effects turn the bottom of the funnel into the top)

**Key Examples:**
- **Fax machine:** "The fax machine works better if your colleagues have one too." Spread not through clever ads but through usage. (Ch. 8)
- **Ethernet (Bob Metcalfe):** Cost of adding each person goes up slowly, but value goes up exponentially. The original Metcalfe's Law graph had just two lines. (Ch. 8)
- **Slack:** Users had a selfish reason to recruit colleagues. "Every day you're not on Slack, people at work are talking about you behind your back." (Ch. 10)
- **Facebook crossing the chasm:** Started at Harvard (insecure high-status students), spread through Ivy League, then to all colleges, then public. At each step, network effects created irresistible tension. (Ch. 19)
- **Water Health International:** Clean water in villages — early adopters talked about it, colorful Jerry cans were visible badges, kids told parents. Status and proximity created a local network effect until the whole village adopted. (Ch. 19)
- **CSA farm share:** Incentive to get friends to join — better variety for everyone. (Ch. 19)

**Actionable Framework:**
1. Design your product so it works BETTER when shared
2. Give early adopters a selfish reason to recruit others
3. Create visible markers of adoption (Jerry cans, badges, shared spaces)
4. "Give them a why. That usually involves changing what you offer."
5. The bridge across the chasm: "What will I tell my friends? Why will I tell them?"
6. "It is never the case that people will tell their friends because you want them to, or because you asked them to"

**Reversal:** Not every product has natural network effects. "There's no reason to talk about a new kind of chocolate you really like. It doesn't make your life better if others eat it." If there's no network effect, you need other growth levers.

### [TIM:NE.1] Crossing the Chasm

**Source:** This Is Marketing (Godin), Chapter 19 (citing Geoff Moore)
**Core Principle:** Early adopters buy because things are new. The mass market buys because things work. This gap — the chasm — kills most products. "In order to satisfy the early adopters, you may just need to annoy the masses." Network effects are the primary bridge across: early adopters have incentive to recruit because it improves their own experience.
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: You have early adopter traction but mass market adoption stalls; you need to understand the gap between first users and mainstream
- AVOID WHEN: You haven't even won early adopters yet — win them first
- COMBINES WITH: `[TIM:NE]` (parent), `[TIM:TN]` (tension drives chasm crossing), `[TIM:PLU]` (mass market needs "people like us" proof)

**Key Examples:**
- **Facebook at Harvard:** Crossed local chasm after local chasm. At each school, neophiliac went first, then status ratchet spread it. "Join in, or face your worst fear — be socially isolated." (Ch. 19)
- **Yo-yo at school:** Wrong kid on Monday — nothing. Charismatic fifth-grader with three extra yo-yos — whole school within weeks. (Ch. 19)
- **Gartner Hype Cycle:** Technology trigger → Peak of inflated expectations → Trough of disillusionment (the chasm) → Slope of enlightenment → Plateau of productivity. (Ch. 19)
- **HugDug failure:** Godin's own project — got trial but failed to find whales, didn't create enough tension for ambassadors, and "we weren't nearly specific enough about who our first customers would be." (Ch. 19)

**Actionable Framework:**
1. First: win the neophiliacs (they like new things)
2. Then: give them tools to recruit others (selfish motivation)
3. Don't try to sell to the mass market the way you sold to early adopters
4. Mass market wants: safe, proven, "people like us do this"
5. "The peer-to-peer movement of ideas is how we cross the chasm"
6. Start with a local chasm, not a global one: "You don't need to cross a global chasm. A local one can change everything."

**Reversal:** "You might not find the bridge." Many products fail at the chasm. HugDug, Godin's own project, is an honest example.

### [TIM:NE.2] LEED Certification (B2B Network Effects)

**Source:** This Is Marketing (Godin), Chapter 19
**Core Principle:** B2B marketing follows the same pattern. "The one question that every business buyer asks herself is: 'What will I tell my boss?'" LEED certification grew from 2 buildings/day to thousands because real estate developers faced status-based pressure: if competitors got certified, skipping it felt defective.
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: Marketing B2B products; enterprise sales; getting organizations to adopt
- AVOID WHEN: You default to "cheapest" as the answer to "What will I tell my boss?"
- COMBINES WITH: `[TIM:NE]` (parent), `[TIM:ST]` (B2B decisions are status decisions), `[TIM:PLU]` ("people like us build certified buildings")

**Actionable Framework:**
1. "What will I tell my boss?" is the core B2B marketing question
2. The lazy answer: "I bought the cheapest one"
3. Better answers involve: status, fear, affiliation, belonging, safety, commitment, insight
4. B2B adoption follows the same early-adopter → chasm → mass market curve
5. Create status pressure so skipping your product feels risky

**Reversal:** B2B is not exempt from emotional marketing. Pretending it's purely rational misses how decisions actually happen.

## 5. [TIM:MV] Movements (Organizing Tribes)

**Source:** This Is Marketing (Godin), Chapters 20-21
**Core Principle:** Marketing that works is about organizing and leading a tribe. It's not about stunts or hype — it's about persistent commitment to change. "Most marketers are fly-by-night operators, knocking on doors and moving on. But some hunker down and commit. And in return, the tribe commits to them." The key: show up, keep showing up, and give the tribe something worth rallying around.
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: You want sustainable community-driven growth; building a brand that lasts; creating cultural change
- AVOID WHEN: You want quick results without long-term commitment; you're treating the community as a means to your ends
- COMBINES WITH: `[TIM:TR]` (tribes are the building blocks of movements), `[TIM:PLU]` (movements normalize behavior), `[TIM:TN]` (movements create tension for non-members), `[TIM:PM]` (movements are built on permission)

**Key Examples:**
- **Zig Ziglar's town strategy:** Instead of making easy sales and driving to the next town, he moved in. Took a room for weeks. Showed up 5-6-7 times to the same people. Crossed the local chasm through persistence, not brilliance. (Ch. 20)
- **NRA (5M members):** "By activating those members and making it easy for them to talk to their friends, they're able to create significant leverage." Members are 2x as likely to contact government officials. (Ch. 21)
- **Tesla:** "Broke the other cars first" — made existing luxury cars feel obsolete for a specific psychographic. Went to difficult extremes: fastest, safest, most efficient. (Ch. 21)
- **Getting an agent (Hollywood):** "The method isn't to go out and find an agent. The method is to do work so impossibly magical that agents come looking for you." (Ch. 21)

**Actionable Framework:**
1. "The best marketers are farmers, not hunters. Plant, tend, plow, fertilize, weed, repeat."
2. Commit to a town/market/community — don't fly by
3. "Give the tribe something to talk about and stand for. Insiders and outsiders."
4. Create shared interests, goals, and language
5. Cultural artifacts: code words, symbols, secret handshakes, Easter eggs
6. Challenge the tribe to go further — Nike's Breaking2 challenge
7. Show up regularly, consistently, and generously — for years

**Reversal:** Movements fade without energy. "There's a half-life at work." The alternative to fading is reinvestment — not seeking the next shiny object.

### [TIM:MV.1] "For People Who Believe..."

**Source:** This Is Marketing (Godin), Chapter 4
**Core Principle:** The simple marketing promise template: "My product is for people who believe ___. I will focus on people who want ___. I promise that engaging with what I make will help you get ___." This frames marketing as serving a belief system, not selling features.
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: Defining your positioning; crafting messaging; deciding who to serve
- AVOID WHEN: You fill in the blanks with "everyone" or "everything"
- COMBINES WITH: `[TIM:SVA]` (the beliefs define the audience), `[TIM:PLU]` (beliefs create identity), `[TIM:TR]` (shared beliefs form tribes)

**Actionable Framework:**
1. Fill in all three blanks. If you can't, you don't have a strategy yet.
2. The "believe" blank is more important than demographics
3. Test: would the people you describe recognize themselves?
4. The promise should be about transformation, not features

**Reversal:** If your "believe" is something everyone believes, it's too broad. If it's something nobody believes, it's too narrow.

## 6. [TIM:SM] Semiotics (Symbols, Shortcuts)

**Source:** This Is Marketing (Godin), Chapter 13
**Core Principle:** "We scan instead of study." When we encounter something new, we ask: "What does this remind me of?" Semiotics is the study of symbols and shortcuts — logos, typefaces, design, packaging, pricing, tone of voice. Busy people don't care about your work as much as you do. They categorize instantly based on familiar signals. "If it reminds us of a scam, it will take a long time to undo that initial impression."
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: Designing any touchpoint (website, packaging, email, store, product); choosing visual identity; wondering why people don't trust you
- AVOID WHEN: You think features alone will convince people; you believe design doesn't matter because your product is great
- COMBINES WITH: `[TIM:ST]` (symbols signal status), `[TIM:PLU]` (symbols define "us"), `[TIM:TT]` (trust is built through familiar symbols)

**Key Examples:**
- **Nike swoosh:** Billions spent to teach millions that the swoosh = human possibility + achievement + status. (Ch. 13)
- **Comic Sans:** For designers, a symbol of bad taste, low status, laziness. (Ch. 13)
- **Hotel room setup:** "If it reminds us of a high school cafeteria, we know how to act. If there are row upon row of hotel-type chairs in straight lines, we know how to sit and act glazed." (Ch. 13)
- **Don LaFontaine (5,000+ movie voice-overs):** His head start compounded — his voice reminded audiences of big-time movies. (Ch. 13)
- **Hoodie in Silicon Valley vs. East London:** Same garment, different signal. Silicon Valley = "I'm too busy for shopping" (status). East London = potentially threatening. (Ch. 13)
- **Witchsy.com "Keith":** Penelope and Kate created a fictional male partner "Keith" whose emails got faster, more respectful responses. Semiotics of gender bias in business communication. (Ch. 13)
- **Nigerian email spam:** Sloppy on purpose — filters out skeptical people. Sends a signal to "people like us" (the gullible). (Ch. 13)

**Actionable Framework:**
1. "What does this remind you of?" — ask about every touchpoint
2. Hire a professional — "What a professional does is design something that OTHER people will like"
3. "Send a signal that feels like a sign we already trust, then change it enough to let us know that it's new"
4. "Most car ads look the same" — sameness signals safety for big investments
5. A brand is a shorthand for expectations. "If people care, you've got a brand."
6. Logo matters less than the promise, but don't be careless — "pick a logo, don't spend a lot on it, and keep it"

**Reversal:** "The symbol is in the mind of the person looking at it" — not the person who made it. Your intent doesn't matter; their interpretation does.

### [TIM:SM.1] Brands and Logos

**Source:** This Is Marketing (Godin), Chapter 13
**Core Principle:** A brand is "a shorthand for the customer's expectations. What promise do they think you're making?" A logo is the Post-it reminder of that promise — without a brand, a logo is meaningless. "If Nike had a hotel, you'd know what it would be like. That's Nike's brand." Most admired logos represent admired brands — we imbue them with brand power.
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: Building brand identity; deciding how much to invest in visual design
- AVOID WHEN: You're spending huge resources on a logo before having a brand promise
- COMBINES WITH: `[TIM:SM]` (parent), `[TIM:TT]` (brand = stored trust)

**Key Examples:**
- **Starbucks mermaid:** "It's possible for a terrible logo to adorn a fabulous brand (complicated mermaid, anyone?)." (Ch. 13)
- **Google, Sephora, Costco:** "Many of the best brands have no identifiable or memorable logo." (Ch. 13)
- **Verizon/AT&T:** "How do we know these brands are worthless? Because if we switched someone from one to the other, they wouldn't care." (Ch. 13)
- **Helvetica logos:** Most major brands use similar typefaces — "not laziness, they're trying to remind you of a solid company." (Ch. 13)

**Actionable Framework:**
1. Build the brand (promise + experience) before worrying about the logo
2. "If you want to build a marketing asset, invest in connection and other nontransferable properties"
3. "Pick a logo, don't spend a ton of money or have a lot of meetings about it, and keep it for as long as you keep your first name"
4. A commodity has no brand — "Get me exactly what I got yesterday, faster and cheaper"

**Reversal:** A great logo on a bad brand = wasted investment. A bad logo on a great brand = negligible damage.

## 7. [TIM:CH.4] Stories

**Source:** This Is Marketing (Godin), Chapters 3, 6
**Core Principle:** We tell stories that resonate and hold up over time. Stories that are true because we made them true with our actions. We make connections (humans are lonely and want to be seen). We create experiences (using a product, engaging with a service — each is part of the story). The organization works for and with the marketer because "marketing is all of it."
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: Crafting messaging; building narrative around your product; understanding why customers buy (hint: it's the story, not the features)
- AVOID WHEN: You think stories are optional decorations on top of features
- COMBINES WITH: `[TIM:SM]` (stories are told through symbols), `[TIM:PLU]` (stories define "us"), `[TIM:ST]` (stories shift status)

**Key Examples:**
- **Quarter-inch drill bit:** "People don't want to buy a quarter-inch drill bit. They want to feel safe and respected." The story goes from drill → hole → shelf → uncluttered room → peace of mind → safety and respect. (Ch. 3)
- **SUV buyers:** "Why do people who will never drive off-road buy a $90,000 Land Cruiser?" Because marketing is about irrational forces. (Ch. 3)
- **Dog food:** Americans spent $24B on dog food. "Dog food is for dog owners. It's for the way it makes them feel." Dogs didn't choose the elk and bison ingredients. (Ch. 5)
- **Bernadette Jiwa's 10 things good stories do:** Connect to purpose, celebrate strengths, deepen unique value, reinforce core values, help value-based decisions, respond to customers, attract aligned customers, build loyalty, attract like-minded employees, keep us motivated. (Ch. 6)

**Actionable Framework:**
1. "Don't begin with machines, inventory, or tactics. Begin with dreams and fears."
2. Find the emotion behind the feature: use the "But wait..." drill (drill → hole → shelf → feeling)
3. "Nobody needs your product" — they need the feeling it provides
4. "We sell feelings, status, and connection, not tasks or stuff"
5. Stories must be true — "stories that are true, because we made them true with our actions"
6. "The goal isn't to personalize the work. It's to make it personal."

**Reversal:** "If you bring your best self to the world and the world doesn't receive it, it's entirely possible that your marketing sucked." A bad story ≠ a bad person. Fix the marketing, not yourself.

### [TIM:CH.5] Dreams and Desires Canvas

**Source:** This Is Marketing (Godin), Chapter 7
**Core Principle:** There are approximately 30 foundational human dreams and desires that marketers work with: adventure, affection, belonging, community, control, creativity, delight, freedom, friendship, health, learning, luxury, nostalgia, participation, peace of mind, power, reassurance, reliability, respect, revenge, romance, safety, security, strength, sympathy, tension, etc. "You could probably add ten more. But it's unlikely you could add fifty more."
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: Defining what emotional change you're offering; mapping your product to human needs; innovating new solutions
- AVOID WHEN: You're focused on features rather than the emotions those features serve
- COMBINES WITH: `[TIM:CH]` (change operates at the emotional level), `[TIM:SVA]` (different audiences prioritize different desires)

**Actionable Framework:**
1. Review the ~30 foundational desires: which 2-3 does your product serve?
2. "Innovative marketers invent new solutions that work with old emotions"
3. Begin with assertions about your audience: what do they talk about when no one is eavesdropping? What do they remember at the end of the day?
4. "Don't begin with what you know how to do. Begin with dreams and fears."

**Reversal:** People confuse wants and needs. They're terrible at inventing new solutions. And they don't all want the same thing. Understanding this triple confusion is the marketer's edge.

## 8. [TIM:CH.6] Driving Change — Practical Imperatives

**Source:** This Is Marketing (Godin), Chapters 5, 12, 14, 15, 22
**Core Principle:** A collection of practical Godin imperatives for making change happen: XY positioning on extremes, enrollment, frequency, goals/strategy/tactics hierarchy, "good enough" shipping, and treating different people differently.
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: Any tactical marketing decision; you need practical frameworks for execution
- AVOID WHEN: Never — these are always applicable
- COMBINES WITH: All other tags

### [TIM:CH.6a] XY Positioning on Extremes

**Source:** This Is Marketing (Godin), Chapter 5
**Core Principle:** Draw a simple XY grid. Pick two attributes people care about. Plot all alternatives. Find a position where you alone are the answer. "Build your own quadrant. Find two axes that have been overlooked." Claims must be true and generous (not invented positions like 1950s soap ads).
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: Defining positioning; competing in a crowded market; need to differentiate
- AVOID WHEN: You pick popular, crowded axes instead of overlooked ones
- COMBINES WITH: `[TIM:SVA]` (extremes serve a specific audience), `[TIM:PR]` (price can be an axis)

**Key Examples:**
- **Diamonds across town:** Speed vs. Security — armored car, postal service, bike messenger each occupy a different quadrant. (Ch. 5)
- **Music teacher:** "Serious + students win competitions" on one axis = worth driving to and paying extra for. Different teacher: "whole student + connection" = completely different quadrant. (Ch. 5)

**Actionable Framework:**
1. Choose two axes (not the most popular — the most overlooked)
2. Plot all alternatives your customer might consider
3. Find the empty quadrant where you're the obvious choice
4. "Choose your extremes and you choose your market. And vice versa."

### [TIM:CH.6b] Goals, Strategy, and Tactics Hierarchy

**Source:** This Is Marketing (Godin), Chapter 15
**Core Principle:** Goal = the change you seek to make (unwavering destination). Strategy = long-lasting investment approach (change reluctantly). Tactics = dozens of steps supporting strategy (change freely). "If you tell competition your tactics, it costs you. If you tell them your strategy, it won't matter — they don't have the guts."
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: Planning; feeling lost in tactical details; evaluating whether to change approach
- AVOID WHEN: You're changing strategy as often as tactics — that's the mistake
- COMBINES WITH: All tags

**Key Examples:**
- **Coca-Cola:** Goal = more people drink more Coke. Strategy = massive ads showing Coke as cultural happiness. Tactics = the specific ads (change regularly). (Ch. 15)
- **Patagonia:** Goal = tiny group of outdoor enthusiasts caring about environment. Strategy = redefine how some people think about environmental impact and clothing. Tactics = recycled materials, brick stores, specific prices. "Same strategy for 30+ years." (Ch. 15)

### [TIM:CH.6c] Enrollment (Not Mandatory Education)

**Source:** This Is Marketing (Godin), Chapter 14
**Core Principle:** "There's no such thing as mandatory education. It's almost impossible to teach people against their will." The alternative: voluntary enrollment — people eagerly lending attention because you earned it. "Enrollment is mutual, consensual, and often leads to change."
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: Trying to get people to engage; they seem resistant; you're pushing instead of pulling
- AVOID WHEN: You're trying to force adoption — that's not marketing, it's coercion
- COMBINES WITH: `[TIM:PM]` (enrollment is the first step to permission), `[TIM:TN]` (tension drives enrollment)

**Actionable Framework:**
1. "Enrollment is hands raised, eyes on the board, notes being taken"
2. Lazy marketers try to buy enrollment with flashy ads
3. Best marketers earn enrollment by seeking people who want the change AND connecting them to each other
4. "We ask people to eagerly lend us their attention. The promise is that it's worth their effort."

### [TIM:CH.6d] The Tyranny of Perfect vs. Good Enough

**Source:** This Is Marketing (Godin), Chapter 22
**Core Principle:** "Perfect closes the door. Better opens the door. Good enough leads to engagement." Good enough → engagement → trust → seeing → learning → promise → enrollment → better. "Ship your work. It's good enough. Then make it better."
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: You're stuck in perfectionism; afraid to launch; waiting for the perfect moment
- AVOID WHEN: You're using "good enough" as excuse for shipping junk (Godin: "viable" means it actually works)
- COMBINES WITH: `[TIM:CH]` (making change requires shipping), `[TIM:TN]` (shipping creates tension that drives improvement)

**Actionable Framework:**
1. "Ship your work. It's good enough. Then make it better."
2. "Realize that as a marketer, the better you are trying to teach or sell is worth far more than what you are charging"
3. "If you hesitate to market your offering properly, it's not that you're being shy. It's that you're stealing."
4. "There's a student who's ready to sign up. If you hesitate, you're letting us down."

**Reversal:** "It's entirely possible that the thing you are marketing satisfies no real demand. Blow it up. Start over. Make something you're proud of." Good enough applies to execution, not to the strategy.

## 9. [TIM:CH.7] The Simple Marketing Worksheet

**Source:** This Is Marketing (Godin), Appendix
**Core Principle:** Godin's complete marketing worksheet in 13 questions. Every marketing effort should be able to answer all of them.

**The Worksheet:**
1. Who's it for?
2. What's it for?
3. What is the worldview of the audience you're seeking to reach?
4. What are they afraid of?
5. What story will you tell? Is it true?
6. What change are you seeking to make?
7. How will it change their status?
8. How will you reach the early adopters and neophiliacs?
9. Why will they tell their friends?
10. What will they tell their friends?
11. Where's the network effect that will propel this forward?
12. What asset are you building?
13. Are you proud of it?

**Actionable Framework:**
1. Answer all 13 questions before launching
2. If you can't answer one, that's your biggest gap
3. Question 13 is the ethical check — if you're not proud, reconsider
4. Revisit quarterly as your understanding deepens

## 10. [TIM:CH.8] Better Business Plan

**Source:** This Is Marketing (Godin), Chapter 12
**Core Principle:** The modern business plan should have five sections: Truth (the world as it is), Assertions (how you'll change it), Alternatives (what if assertions fail), People (team + customers), and Money. "The purpose of capitalism is to build our culture," not the other way around.
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: Writing a business plan; pitching; planning a new venture
- AVOID WHEN: You use the business plan to "obfuscate, bore, and show ability to comply with expectations"
- COMBINES WITH: `[TIM:CH]` (assertions = change), `[TIM:SVA]` (people section = your SVA)

**Actionable Framework:**
1. **Truth:** Market, needs, competitors, standards, how others succeeded/failed — specific and visceral
2. **Assertions:** "We will do X, and then Y will happen" — the heart of the plan
3. **Alternatives:** "If assertions fail, what do we do?" — flexibility and resilience
4. **People:** Team attitudes/abilities + customer worldviews and status beliefs
5. **Money:** How much, how spent, cash flow, profit/loss, margins, exit

**Reversal:** "A glib reverse engineering of your mission statement isn't helpful." Your "why" is universal (be successful, make money). Your "what and for whom" is what matters.

## 11. [TIM:CH.9] Anti-Spam, Anti-Manipulation Ethics

**Source:** This Is Marketing (Godin), Chapters 1, 17, 23
**Core Principle:** Godin is unequivocally anti-spam, anti-manipulation. "Your emergency is not a license to steal my attention. Your insecurity is not a permit to hustle me or my friends." Marketing is ethical when both marketer and consumer are aware of what's happening and satisfied with the outcome. "Just because you can market something doesn't mean you should."
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: Always — this is a filter on all recommendations
- AVOID WHEN: Never compromise on this
- COMBINES WITH: All tags — every recommendation must pass the ethics filter

**Key Quotes:**
- "Marketing is the generous act of helping someone solve a problem. Their problem."
- "I think it's evil to persuade kids to start smoking."
- "If you don't market the change you'd like to contribute, you're stealing."
- "Marketing is beautiful when it persuades people to get a polio vaccine or to wash their hands before performing surgery."
- "Just because you can market something doesn't mean you should. You've got the power, so you're responsible."

**Actionable Framework:**
1. Ethics test: Are both parties aware of what's happening? Both satisfied with the outcome?
2. "The even better news is that ethical, public marketing will eventually defeat the kind that depends on the shadows."
3. Final worksheet question: "Are you proud of it?"
4. "We can do work that matters for people who care."

**Reversal:** If you need to hide your methods, you're on the wrong path. Transparency is the long-term winning strategy.
