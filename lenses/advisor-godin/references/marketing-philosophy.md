> Tag prefix: `[TIM`

## 1. [TIM:CH] Change — The Only Goal of Marketing

**Source:** This Is Marketing (Godin), Chapters 1-3, Author's Note
**Core Principle:** Marketing is the generous act of helping someone solve a problem. Their problem. The first step is to invent a thing worth making. The second is to design and build it for a specific few. The third is to tell a story that matches their narrative. The fourth is to spread the word. The last is to show up regularly, consistently, and generously for years. Marketing is not about hype, spam, or coercion — it is about making change happen.
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: Starting any marketing initiative; you need to ground your strategy in purpose; you're tempted to "get the word out" before doing the foundational work
- AVOID WHEN: You haven't yet built something worth making; you're reverse-engineering a "change" to justify selling existing inventory
- COMBINES WITH: `[TIM:SVA]` (change requires a specific audience), `[TIM:TN]` (change requires tension), `[TIM:TT]` (change requires trust)

**Key Examples:**
- **VisionSpring glasses in India:** One small change — giving glasses to customers before asking for payment instead of making them choose from a table — doubled sales. Marketing is understanding the change from the customer's perspective, not yours. (Ch. 3)
- **By the Way Bakery:** "We want to make sure no one is left out." Their change: turning hosts from exclusive to inclusive, and guests from outsiders to insiders. (Ch. 4)
- **Penguin Magic:** Built a community by changing the culture of amateur magicians — encouraging creation, review, and peer learning. Their videos have been seen over a billion times with zero ad spend. (Ch. 1)

**Actionable Framework:**
1. Ask: "What change am I trying to make?" — be specific and honest
2. Avoid Stumble 1: grandiose impossible changes that serve as excuses
3. Avoid Stumble 2: reverse-engineering a change to justify what you already sell
4. Define the promise: "If you do X, you will get Y"
5. Ensure the change you seek is worth the journey for those you serve
6. "Marketing is the act of making change happen. Making is insufficient. You haven't made an impact until you've changed someone."

**Reversal:** If your "change" is actually just "buy my product," you're not doing marketing — you're doing advertising from the old playbook. Change must be genuine, not a repackaged sales pitch.

### [TIM:CH.1] Marketing in Five Steps

**Source:** This Is Marketing (Godin), Chapter 2
**Core Principle:** All effective marketing follows five steps in order: (1) Invent a thing worth making with a story worth telling, (2) Design it for a few who will particularly benefit, (3) Tell a story matching the narrative of the smallest viable market, (4) Spread the word, (5) Show up regularly and generously for years to earn permission and enrollment.
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: Planning any marketing effort; auditing why current marketing isn't working (you probably skipped a step)
- AVOID WHEN: Never — this is the foundational sequence
- COMBINES WITH: `[TIM:SVA]` (step 2-3), `[TIM:PM]` (step 5), `[TIM:PLU]` (step 3)

**Key Examples:**
- **Open Heart Project:** Susan Piver followed all five steps — found a real need (meditation community post-retreat), built for smallest viable market (travelers wanting ongoing practice), told a matching story, let word spread organically, and showed up consistently. Result: largest meditation community in the world. (Ch. 4)

**Actionable Framework:**
1. Step 1: "Is this worth making?" — if not, stop here
2. Step 2: "Who specifically will benefit the most?" — choose them
3. Step 3: "What story resonates with their existing worldview?" — match, don't change
4. Step 4: "How will they tell others?" — design for spreadability
5. Step 5: "Am I willing to do this for years?" — if not, reconsider

**Reversal:** Most marketers start at Step 4 ("How do I get the word out?") — Godin insists this is "precisely the wrong question" and must come near the end.

### [TIM:CH.2] Market-Driven vs. Marketing-Driven

**Source:** This Is Marketing (Godin), Chapter 3
**Core Principle:** Being marketing-driven means obsessing over offers, surface shine, and squeezing out one more dollar. Being market-driven means listening to the market, understanding hopes and dreams, and bending the culture toward better. "Being market-driven lasts."
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: You're focused on logos, ad optimization, and pricing tricks at the expense of understanding customers
- AVOID WHEN: Never — always choose market-driven
- COMBINES WITH: `[TIM:SVA]` (market-driven starts with audience), `[TIM:TT]` (market-driven builds trust)

**Key Examples:**
- **Ron Johnson at JCPenney:** He was marketing-driven — imposing his luxury worldview on Penney's bargain-hunting customers. Sales plummeted 50%+. He was trying to transform customers into his kind of shoppers. (Ch. 4)
- **Starbucks vs. Dunkin':** Both sell coffee, but each is market-driven for their respective audiences — precise sets of beliefs about coffee, time, money, community, and luxury. (Ch. 4)

**Actionable Framework:**
1. Ask: "Am I designing for my worldview or theirs?"
2. Listen to what customers tell each other, not just what they tell you
3. Invest in understanding the culture you seek to change
4. "When you're market-driven, you think a lot about the hopes and dreams of your customers and their friends"

**Reversal:** Being market-driven does NOT mean doing whatever customers ask for. Crowds are terrible at inventing breakthroughs. It means understanding their emotional world and serving it.

### [TIM:CH.3] The Simple Marketing Promise Template

**Source:** This Is Marketing (Godin), Chapter 4
**Core Principle:** Every effective marketing effort can be distilled to three sentences: "My product is for people who believe ___. I will focus on people who want ___. I promise that engaging with what I make will help you get ___."
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: Starting a new product/campaign; can't articulate who it's for; need clarity before execution
- AVOID WHEN: Never — always be able to fill these blanks
- COMBINES WITH: `[TIM:SVA]` (the "who"), `[TIM:PLU]` (the "believe"), `[TIM:ST]` (the status shift)

**Actionable Framework:**
1. Fill in: "My product is for people who believe ___"
2. Fill in: "I will focus on people who want ___"
3. Fill in: "I promise that engaging with what I make will help you get ___"
4. If you can't fill all three, you don't have a marketing strategy yet

**Reversal:** If your blanks are too broad ("everyone," "the best," "everything"), start over. Specificity is bravery.

## 2. [TIM:SVA] Smallest Viable Audience

**Source:** This Is Marketing (Godin), Chapters 4, 8
**Core Principle:** The relentless pursuit of mass will make you boring, because mass means average. Begin with the smallest viable market — the minimum number of people you need to influence to make it worth the effort. "The smallest viable market is the focus that, ironically and delightfully, leads to your growth." This is not about settling for less; it's about finding the people who will understand you and fall in love with where you hope to take them.
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: Defining your audience; you're spread too thin; you're getting rejected by "everyone"; you need to make hard decisions about who NOT to serve
- AVOID WHEN: You've already found your SVA and need to expand (but expand by letting them bring others, not by diluting your message)
- COMBINES WITH: `[TIM:PLU]` (SVA must share an identity), `[TIM:TR]` (SVA becomes a tribe), `[TIM:NE]` (SVA grows through network effects), `[TIM:TN]` (SVA creates tension that attracts more)

**Key Examples:**
- **Union Square Cafe (Danny Meyer):** Could only serve 600 people/day. Chose WHICH 600 — people most open to the experience, most likely to tell the right others. Built a restaurant empire from careful curation. (Ch. 4)
- **Grateful Dead:** One top-40 hit. Ever. But $350M+ in concert revenue while Jerry Garcia was alive. They focused all energy on true fans, let them tape shows, relied on small number who supported them a lot. "In 1972, only 5,000 people came to a typical show." (Ch. 8)
- **Congressional campaign:** Only needed 5,000 votes out of 724,000 residents. 5,000 is very different from "everyone." (Ch. 4)
- **Open Heart Project (Susan Piver):** 20,000 members from thousands of small swoops — one full-time staff member running the largest meditation community in the world. (Ch. 4)

**Actionable Framework:**
1. Ask: "What's the minimum number of people I would need to influence to make it worth the effort?"
2. If you could only change 30 people (or 3,000), WHO would they be?
3. Choose people based on what they dream, believe, and want (psychographics), not demographics
4. Find a corner of the market that can't wait for your attention
5. Go to their extremes — find a position where you alone are the perfect answer
6. Overwhelm their wants and dreams with care, attention, and focus
7. Make change so profound they can't help but talk about it
8. "Everything gets easier when you walk away from the hubris of everyone"

**Reversal:** "Smallest viable" does not mean "smallest possible." The audience must be viable — large enough to sustain you. And "viable" means they actually adopt and evangelize, not just that they exist.

### [TIM:SVA.1] "It's Not for You"

**Source:** This Is Marketing (Godin), Chapter 4
**Core Principle:** The ability to say "It's not for you" is a sign of respect — both for the people you're not serving (you won't waste their time) and for the people you are serving (you made this specifically for them). "What matters is whether you've changed the people who trust you."
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: You receive criticism from people outside your SVA; you're tempted to dilute your message to please critics; you need courage to maintain focus
- AVOID WHEN: The criticism is from your actual audience — that's advice worth hearing
- COMBINES WITH: `[TIM:SVA]` (prerequisite), `[TIM:PLU]` (reinforces identity)

**Key Examples:**
- **The comedian and the Italian tour group:** A great comic bombed — not because his material was bad, but because the audience didn't speak English. "It's not for you." (Ch. 4)
- **Amazon one-star reviews:** Every bestselling book has one-star reviews. 12% of Harry Potter reviews gave it 1-2 stars. There's always a desired audience and an accidental audience. (Ch. 8)
- **Nigerian email scams:** The sloppy spelling is intentional — it filters out skeptical, well-informed people. "It's not for you" is a signal. (Ch. 13)

**Actionable Framework:**
1. Accept that every best-selling product will have detractors
2. Distinguish between criticism from your SVA (valuable advice) and criticism from outsiders (irrelevant)
3. Practice saying: "I'm sorry, this isn't for you. Here's the phone number of my competitor."
4. Freedom to ignore critics who don't get it = privilege to polish your story for those who need to hear it

**Reversal:** "It's not for you" is not an excuse for poor quality. If people in your target audience are unhappy, that's a different problem.

### [TIM:SVA.2] 1,000 True Fans

**Source:** This Is Marketing (Godin), Chapter 8 (citing Kevin Kelly, 2008)
**Core Principle:** For independent creators, roughly 1,000 true fans who will buy anything you produce can be sufficient for a better-than-decent living. A true fan drives 200 miles to see you sing, buys every format of your book, pays for the best-of DVD. The challenge isn't winning the mass market — it's the micro market. "It's way more productive to matter to a few instead."
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: You're an independent creator; you're chasing mass market when you don't have 50 people who'd miss you if you were gone; you need to redefine what success looks like
- AVOID WHEN: You have a business model that genuinely requires mass adoption (network-effect platforms)
- COMBINES WITH: `[TIM:SVA]` (1,000 fans IS an SVA), `[TIM:PM]` (fans give you permission), `[TIM:TR]` (fans form a tribe)

**Key Examples:**
- **Grateful Dead:** Relied on a small number of true fans who supported them a lot, rather than a large number who supported them a little. (Ch. 8)
- **Hamilton:** Even at full capacity on Broadway, only a few thousand see it. Its soundtrack sold a few hundred thousand copies. "Our hits aren't hits anymore... they are meaningful for a few and invisible to the rest." (Ch. 8)

**Actionable Framework:**
1. Can you identify 50 people who would miss you if you were gone?
2. If not, that's your first milestone — not a million followers
3. Build for them: "Instead of looking for members for your work, look for ways to do work for your members"
4. One true fan supporting you deeply > 1,000 casual followers

**Reversal:** "Taylor Swift is not your role model." Becoming a hit machine is a different playbook. Someone will be the hit machine — it's probably not going to be you. The other path — connection, empathy, change — is more accessible and more rewarding.

### [TIM:SVA.3] Coloring the Pool Purple

**Source:** This Is Marketing (Godin), Chapter 4
**Core Principle:** A teaspoon of thief-detector dye will turn a swimming pool permanently purple. Drop it in the ocean and no one notices. Your work, no matter how extraordinary, won't make a difference if you drop it in the ocean. "Walk away from the ocean and look for a large swimming pool."
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: You're launching something and trying to reach everyone; your message is getting lost in noise
- AVOID WHEN: You've already dominated a pool and are ready for the next one
- COMBINES WITH: `[TIM:SVA]` (find your pool), `[TIM:NE]` (let your pool overflow into the next)

**Actionable Framework:**
1. Find your swimming pool (specific, bounded community)
2. Focus obsessively until the pool is colored
3. Let your best customers spread to the next pool
4. Repeat

**Reversal:** Starting with the ocean feels ambitious but is almost always a waste. Start small, dominate, expand.

## 3. [TIM:PLU] "People Like Us Do Things Like This"

**Source:** This Is Marketing (Godin), Chapter 9
**Core Principle:** For most of us, our actions are primarily driven by one question: "Do people like me do things like this?" This is how culture works. Normalization creates culture, culture drives choices, which leads to more normalization. Marketers don't make average stuff for average people — they make change by normalizing new behaviors. The marketer's first job is to define "us."
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: You need to understand why people buy (or don't); you want to change behavior; you need to position your product as a cultural norm for a specific group
- AVOID WHEN: You're trying to use this manipulatively — identity-based marketing works only when the identity claim is authentic and generous
- COMBINES WITH: `[TIM:SVA]` (who is "us"?), `[TIM:ST]` (status drives the identity), `[TIM:TN]` (tension comes from seeing "people like us" doing something new), `[TIM:TR]` (tribes are built on shared identity)

**Key Examples:**
- **Blue Ribbons school budget vote:** Instead of arguing, activists tied blue ribbons to trees. Message: "People like us, people in our town, support our schools." Budget passed 2-to-1. (Ch. 9)
- **Crickets vs. beef:** If it's not genetic, why do crickets make us squirm while cows make us hungry? Because people like us eat things like this. (Ch. 9)
- **Robin Hood Foundation ($101M in one night):** Cash for status. Peer pressure of "people like us do things like this" among wealthy Wall Street investors. (Ch. 9)
- **Gay marriage in Ireland:** Parents of a gay son made a video. "People like us" — parents, traditionalists, Irishmen — support our children. Political change is cultural change, and culture changes horizontally, person to person. (Ch. 9)

**Actionable Framework:**
1. Define your "us" — the more specific, connected, and tight, the better
2. Ask: "What do people like us do?" — then normalize the behavior you seek
3. Cultural change flows horizontally (person to person), not vertically (organization to customer)
4. "Normalization creates culture, and culture drives our choices, which leads to more normalization"
5. "We can't change THE culture, but each of us has the opportunity to change A culture — our little pocket of the world"

**Reversal:** "People like us" can also enforce the status quo ("people like us don't take risks"). Understanding this force means you know when you need tension to overcome it.

### [TIM:PLU.1] Internal Narrative

**Source:** This Is Marketing (Godin), Chapter 9
**Core Principle:** Everyone acts in accordance with their internal narratives. You can't get someone to do something they don't want to do. The real question is: where does the internal narrative come from, and how does it get changed? The answer: desire to fit in (people like us) and perception of status (affiliation and dominance).
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: You're puzzled why rational arguments don't work; someone isn't buying what seems obviously good
- AVOID WHEN: You're trying to override someone's narrative with facts — it won't work
- COMBINES WITH: `[TIM:ST]` (status shapes the narrative), `[TIM:TN]` (tension can shift the narrative)

**Actionable Framework:**
1. Map the internal narrative of the people you seek to serve
2. Don't try to change it directly — dance with it
3. "We need to be generous enough to share a story, so they can take action that they'll be proud of"

**Reversal:** You cannot argue someone out of their narrative. "If we can accept that people have embraced who they have become, it gets a lot easier to dance with them."

### [TIM:PLU.2] Elite vs. Exclusive

**Source:** This Is Marketing (Godin), Chapter 9
**Core Principle:** Elite is external — does the world respect this badge? Exclusive is internal — us vs. them, insiders vs. outsiders. "Exclusive organizations thrive as long as their members wish to belong." Harvard Business School is both; the Rhodes Scholarship is elite but not exclusive; the Hell's Angels are exclusive but not elite.
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: Building community or membership; deciding what kind of status to offer
- AVOID WHEN: You want broad mass adoption immediately — exclusivity slows initial growth
- COMBINES WITH: `[TIM:TR]` (tribes are exclusive), `[TIM:ST]` (exclusivity = status)

**Actionable Framework:**
1. Exclusive organizations change things more than elite ones
2. "We have no control over our elite status, and it can be taken away in an instant"
3. Build exclusive: every member is "people like us" — sign up and gain status, walk away and lose it
4. "In order to change a culture, we begin with an exclusive cohort"

**Reversal:** Elite without exclusive is fragile. Exclusive without delivery becomes a scam.

## 4. [TIM:ST] Status (Affiliation vs. Dominance)

**Source:** This Is Marketing (Godin), Chapter 11
**Core Principle:** Status is our position in the hierarchy and our perception of that position. The desire to change our status, or to protect it, drives almost everything we do. There are two fundamentally different ways to measure status: affiliation (horizontal — "Who's standing next to me?" "Who trusts you?" "Can't we all get along?") and dominion (vertical — "This is mine, not yours." "Who has more power?" "I did this myself."). Understanding which lens your customer uses is critical.
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: You're making any offer that involves a decision — every big decision is made based on perceptions of status; you need to understand why someone isn't buying; you want to design the right appeal
- AVOID WHEN: You assume everyone wants higher status (many fight to maintain or even lower their status)
- COMBINES WITH: `[TIM:PLU]` (status defines "us"), `[TIM:TN]` (tension is about status change), `[TIM:PR]` (price signals status), `[TIM:SM]` (symbols signal status)

**Key Examples:**
- **The Godfather opening scene:** Bonasera (low status) visits Don Corleone (high status). The tension arises when status roles are challenged. The scene resolves only when the normal hierarchy is restored. (Ch. 11)
- **Maasai lion guardians:** Conservation biologist Leela Hazzah didn't try to argue against lion-killing. She created a new rite of passage — protecting lions — that provided the same status outcome (bravery, passage to manhood) through a different behavior. (Ch. 11)
- **Uber:** Built its brand on dominance signals — contentious relationships with governments, competitors, drivers. This aligned with users/investors who respond to winning/losing narratives. (Ch. 11)
- **Frank Sinatra:** Externally high status (suave, sophisticated). Internally low status (skinny kid, disrespected). "We don't know if the person we're engaging with appears to have high status and doesn't believe it." (Ch. 11)

**Actionable Framework:**
1. For each person you serve, determine: Do they measure dominion or affiliation?
2. Dominion asks: "Who has more power? This is mine. My side winning."
3. Affiliation asks: "Who knows you? Who trusts you? Where do you stand within the tribe?"
4. "Dominance is a vertical experience, above or below. Affiliation is a horizontal one: Who's standing next to me?"
5. Design your appeal to match their measurement system — don't impose yours

**Reversal:** "It's a mistake to believe that everyone wants to make their status higher. In fact, few people do." Some people fight to maintain or lower their status because lower status = safety, less competition.

### [TIM:ST.1] Six Things About Status

**Source:** This Is Marketing (Godin), Chapter 11
**Core Principle:** (1) Status is always relative, not absolute. (2) Status is in the eyes of the beholder — different observers see different status. (3) Status attended to is the status that matters. (4) Status has inertia — we work harder to maintain than to change. (5) Status is learned — but cohort can shift it quickly. (6) Shame is the status killer — it undermines the entire narrative.
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: Analyzing why someone resists or embraces change; designing status-aware marketing
- AVOID WHEN: Never — these six facts are always in play
- COMBINES WITH: All `[TIM:ST]` sub-tags

**Actionable Framework:**
1. Remember: "6 is bigger than 4, but lower than 11. There is no highest number."
2. Status inertia means existing customers will resist change even when it benefits them
3. Shame is a powerful lever — but use it ethically, because it destroys trust when weaponized
4. "We adjust our status constantly, intuitively playing with it based on the situation"

**Reversal:** Using shame to manipulate is effective short-term but destructive long-term. Godin's anti-manipulation stance means status should be used to empower, not exploit.

### [TIM:ST.2] Status Quadrants

**Source:** This Is Marketing (Godin), Chapter 11
**Core Principle:** People can be mapped on two grids: (1) External status (high/low) vs. Internal status (high/low), producing four archetypes. (2) Direction of movement (seeking to raise vs. lower) vs. Focus (self vs. others), producing four behavior patterns: philanthropist (raises others, raises self), self-effacing (raises others, lowers self), narcissist (lowers others, lowers self), hard-charging competitor (raises self, lowers others).
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: Profiling your target customer's status behavior; designing appeal that matches their pattern
- AVOID WHEN: Over-simplifying real people into one quadrant — people shift between quadrants situationally
- COMBINES WITH: `[TIM:ST]` (parent), `[TIM:PLU]` (identity quadrant)

**Actionable Framework:**
1. Quadrant (a): High external + high internal = confident chooser (rare — Oprah)
2. Quadrant (b): Low external + high internal = artistic drive, willing to strive, risk of bitterness
3. Quadrant (c): Low external + low internal = consistent role, resistant to change (Cinderella before the ball)
4. Quadrant (d): High external + low internal = impostor syndrome, diva behavior (Frank Sinatra)
5. Match your marketing to the quadrant your audience lives in

**Reversal:** People are not static in one quadrant. The same person may be (a) at work and (c) at home. Context matters.

## 5. [TIM:TN] Tension (Creating Forward Motion)

**Source:** This Is Marketing (Godin), Chapter 10
**Core Principle:** Marketing creates tension — the discomfort of the status quo becoming unacceptable. Tension is not fear. Fear paralyzes; tension motivates forward motion. "Tension is the hallmark of a great educational experience." Marketers create tension, and forward motion relieves that tension. "If you care enough about the change you seek to make, you will care enough to generously and respectfully create tension on behalf of that change."
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: People understand your offer but aren't acting; the status quo is comfortable; you need to create urgency without manipulation
- AVOID WHEN: You're creating fear instead of tension; you're coercing rather than inviting; your audience is already overwhelmed
- COMBINES WITH: `[TIM:ST]` (the engine of tension is status), `[TIM:PLU]` (tension from "people like us are doing this and you're not"), `[TIM:NE]` (network effects create "left behind" tension)

**Key Examples:**
- **Slack adoption:** Early adopters used it. Then colleagues said "You're missing out." Every day not on Slack = people talking about you, working without you. Tension relieved by signing in. (Ch. 10)
- **Las Vegas casinos:** New fancy casinos created tension for visitors to older ones. "Am I the sort of person who goes to a casino this run-down?" Existence of a fancier alternative degraded their current experience. (Ch. 10)
- **Going-out-of-business sales:** Create tension of "What bargains did I miss?" even though buying from a closing store is irrational. (Ch. 10)
- **Supreme sneakers (250 pairs):** Scarcity + peer behavior = intense tension. "I'm getting a pair — are you coming?" (Ch. 10)

**Actionable Framework:**
1. Pattern match (easy adoption) vs. Pattern interrupt (requires tension) — know which you need
2. Pattern match: your offering fits existing behavior ("another cereal brand")
3. Pattern interrupt: you need tension to break existing patterns
4. Best time for pattern interrupt: when people don't have a pattern yet (new platform, new parents, people who just moved)
5. Create tension through: scarcity, social proof, peer behavior, status gaps, FOMO
6. Always ask: "What will relieve this tension?" — make the answer "taking the action I want them to take"

**Reversal:** "If you feel like you're coercing people, manipulating them or causing them to be afraid, you're probably doing it wrong." Tension motivates; fear freezes.

### [TIM:TN.1] Pattern Match vs. Pattern Interrupt

**Source:** This Is Marketing (Godin), Chapter 10
**Core Principle:** Pattern match is business as usual — your offering fits the existing story, pace, and risk level. Pattern interrupt requires a jolt — tension is created, energy is diverted to consider this new input. Most people, most of the time, will say "no" to a pattern interrupt. The best time for a pattern interrupt is when people don't have a pattern yet.
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: Deciding how to introduce your product; understanding why adoption is slow
- AVOID WHEN: Never — every offering is either a match or an interrupt
- COMBINES WITH: `[TIM:TN]` (interrupt requires tension), `[TIM:NE]` (network effects turn matches into interrupts for non-adopters)

**Key Examples:**
- **Slack:** Started as pattern match (new software for people who like new software), then created pattern interrupts peer-to-peer. (Ch. 10)
- **Breakfast cereal switching:** Pattern match — easy to swap one brand for another. (Ch. 10)
- **Hiring a gardener for the first time:** Pattern interrupt — requires undoing the status quo of "I don't hire gardeners." (Ch. 10)

**Actionable Framework:**
1. Classify your offering: match or interrupt?
2. If match: compete on story, positioning, and fit
3. If interrupt: you need tension. Where will it come from?
4. Target people without existing patterns (new dads, engaged women, new movers, new platform users)
5. Give early adopters tools to create interrupts for others (Slack gave users a reason to tell colleagues)

**Reversal:** Not every situation calls for an interrupt. If you can win with a pattern match, that's easier and cheaper.

### [TIM:TN.2] What Are You Breaking?

**Source:** This Is Marketing (Godin), Chapter 10
**Core Principle:** Every new project breaks something. The existence of an alternative causes something to no longer be true. "When you launch the second hotel in Niagara Falls, the first hotel is no longer the one and only." Tension comes from the realization that something is now broken.
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: Launching something new; understanding the tension your arrival creates
- AVOID WHEN: You're unaware of what you're disrupting — always be intentional
- COMBINES WITH: `[TIM:TN]` (breaking creates tension), `[TIM:ST]` (breaking shifts status)

**Actionable Framework:**
1. What is no longer true because you exist?
2. Who loses status because of your arrival?
3. "This is what tension feels like. The tension of being left behind."
4. "Marketers who cause change cause tension."

**Reversal:** If your arrival breaks nothing, you haven't created tension, and adoption will be slow.

## 6. [TIM:TT] Trust

**Source:** This Is Marketing (Godin), Chapters 10, 17, 18
**Core Principle:** Trust is as scarce as attention. In a world where more people are connected but fewer are trusted, a trusted marketer earns enrollment, can make and keep promises, and earns the right to tell a story uninterrupted. Trust comes from action, not words. "We remember what you did long after we forget what you said." Frequency breeds trust — the familiar is normal and the normal is trusted. The goal is to be famous to the tribe, not to everyone.
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: Building long-term relationships; you need to earn the right to educate; you want sustainable growth
- AVOID WHEN: You're looking for a quick win — trust takes time; trying to fake trust with PR
- COMBINES WITH: `[TIM:PM]` (trust enables permission), `[TIM:SVA]` (trust within the tribe matters most), `[TIM:FN]` (trust moves people through the funnel)

**Key Examples:**
- **Everyone is famous to 1,500 people.** Some to 3,000. Being known to the right 3,000 people is plenty for a consultant, designer, or inventor. "The goal isn't to maximize your social media numbers. The goal is to be known to the smallest viable audience." (Ch. 18)
- **Jay Levinson's frequency rule:** "Don't change your ads when you're tired of them. Don't even change them when your friends are tired of them. Change them when your accountant is tired of them." (Ch. 15)
- **Zig Ziglar:** Instead of making easy early-adopter sales and moving on, he moved into a town. He stayed. He showed up 5, 6, 7 times to the same person. That's how he crossed the local chasm — with frequency and trust. (Ch. 20)

**Actionable Framework:**
1. Build trust through action, not words: "Talking means focusing on press conferences. Not talking means focusing on what you do when no one is watching."
2. Frequency = trust: show up regularly, consistently, generously, for years
3. "The market has been trained to associate frequency with trust"
4. Three paths in a trust-scarce world: Ignored → Sneaking around → Trusted. Only trusted pays off.
5. "The simplest definition of permission is the people who would miss you if you didn't reach out."

**Reversal:** Trust invested in poorly can be destroyed in an instant. "Lowering your price doesn't make you more trusted. It does the opposite." Cognitive dissonance means people invent trust stories around high-cost investments.

### [TIM:TT.1] Trust of Action

**Source:** This Is Marketing (Godin), Chapter 18
**Core Principle:** In a world that scans instead of reads, the best way to earn trust is through action. "We remember what you did long after we forget what you said." When you refunded the defective product, when you lost data and responded, when you had to close the plant — what did you DO?
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: Anytime you face a trust decision — customer complaints, crises, everyday interactions
- AVOID WHEN: Never — always choose action over words
- COMBINES WITH: `[TIM:TT]` (parent), `[TIM:PM]` (actions earn permission)

**Actionable Framework:**
1. "Marketers spend a lot of time talking. We need to spend far more time DOING."
2. "Not talking means focusing on what you do when no one is watching, one person at a time, day by day."
3. Have the CEO call the triple-charged customer. Drive across town. Get to the last FedEx pickup.
4. Amazement and delight go a long way — especially for your best customers.

**Reversal:** If your actions contradict your words, the words are worse than useless — they become evidence of dishonesty.

### [TIM:TT.2] Famous to the Tribe

**Source:** This Is Marketing (Godin), Chapter 18
**Core Principle:** "Everyone is famous to fifteen hundred people." When 3,000 or 10,000 people think you're famous, it changes things — not just because they've heard of you, but because people they trust have heard of you too. "If you're a business consultant, a designer, or an inventor, being famous to the right three thousand people is plenty."
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: You're chasing broad fame when you should be deepening tribe fame; measuring wrong metrics (follower count vs. tribe recognition)
- AVOID WHEN: You genuinely need mass awareness (rare)
- COMBINES WITH: `[TIM:SVA]` (smallest viable audience = your fame pool), `[TIM:TR]` (the tribe validates your fame)

**Actionable Framework:**
1. "The goal isn't to maximize your social media numbers. The goal is to be known to the smallest viable audience."
2. Being famous to 3,000 right people > being known to 300,000 wrong people
3. Fame compounds within a tribe — when peers validate you, trust accelerates

**Reversal:** Broad fame is expensive and fragile. Tribal fame is earned and durable.

## 7. [TIM:TR] Tribes

**Source:** This Is Marketing (Godin), Chapter 20
**Core Principle:** A tribe is a group of people connected to one another, connected to a leader, and connected to an idea. "It's not your tribe" — the tribe doesn't belong to you. If you're fortunate, they'll listen. If you invest in them, they'll show you what they want. "The tribe would probably survive if you went away. The goal is for them to miss you if you did." Tribes need shared interests, shared goals, and shared language.
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: You have an audience and want to deepen connection; you want to create a movement, not just a customer base; you need sustainable community-driven growth
- AVOID WHEN: You treat the tribe as a tool for your own aims; you're a fly-by-night operator not willing to commit
- COMBINES WITH: `[TIM:SVA]` (tribe = your SVA organized), `[TIM:PLU]` (tribe = "people like us"), `[TIM:MV]` (tribes become movements), `[TIM:NE]` (tribes grow through network effects)

**Key Examples:**
- **NRA (5 million members):** Less than 2% of the population, but massive impact. Comfortable saying "It's not for you." Activate members to talk to friends. Embrace "people like us." (Ch. 21)
- **Alcoholics Anonymous:** Built on peer connection. "When in doubt, talk about AA, because talking about it is a generous act. It's a shame killer. It's a life raft." (Ch. 17)

**Actionable Framework:**
1. "The tribe doesn't belong to you" — you're a leader in service, not an owner
2. Create cultural artifacts: code words, symbols, secret handshakes, Easter eggs
3. "Don't say it all, and don't make it obvious" — reward commitment and longevity
4. Challenge the tribe to go further, adopt goals, push forward
5. Commit. "Most marketers are fly-by-night operators. Some hunker down and commit. And in return, the tribe commits to them."
6. "The best marketers are farmers, not hunters. Plant, tend, plow, fertilize, weed, repeat."

**Reversal:** Tribes fade without energy. "There's a half-life at work. For any tribal behavior that's not energetically maintained, half of the activity will disappear." Reinvest constantly.

### [TIM:TR.1] Story of Self, Story of Us, Story of Now

**Source:** This Is Marketing (Godin), Chapter 20 (citing Marshall Ganz)
**Core Principle:** Marshall Ganz articulated a three-step narrative for action: (1) Story of Self — your transition, giving you standing to speak. "You are people like us." (2) Story of Us — the kernel of a tribe. "Why should we care?" (3) Story of Now — the critical pivot. "The urgency of now requires that we do it together."
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: Launching a movement; rallying a tribe; making a pitch; building community narrative
- AVOID WHEN: You don't have an authentic story of self (don't fake it)
- COMBINES WITH: `[TIM:TR]` (parent), `[TIM:PLU]` (story of us = identity), `[TIM:TN]` (story of now = tension)

**Key Examples:**
- **Figure skating story:** "I was 50 pounds overweight... I discovered competitive figure skating... I'm so glad you came to the rink today." Self → Us → Now, with a clear call to action. (Ch. 20)

**Actionable Framework:**
1. Story of Self: "I was like you. I was in the desert. Then I learned something and now I'm here."
2. Story of Us: "I did not do this alone. I see in you the very pain I saw in myself. Together, we can make this better."
3. Story of Now: "If we hesitate, it won't work. The urgency of now requires that we do it together."
4. The three stories flow naturally and end with a specific call to action

**Reversal:** "It's not about catastrophizing your situation or the faux empathy of online vulnerability." The story of self must be genuine, not performative.

### [TIM:TR.2] Manipulation Is the Tribe Killer

**Source:** This Is Marketing (Godin), Chapter 20
**Core Principle:** Godin reverses Saul Alinsky's "Rules for Radicals" to create positive tribe leadership rules. Instead of "Ridicule is man's most potent weapon" → "Treat others the way you'd want to be treated." Instead of "Pick the target, freeze it, personalize it, polarize it" → "Celebrate your people, free them to do even more, invite everyone along. Disagree with institutions, not people."
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: You're tempted to use divisive, zero-sum tactics; building long-term community
- AVOID WHEN: Never — Godin's anti-manipulation stance is absolute
- COMBINES WITH: `[TIM:TR]` (parent), `[TIM:TT]` (trust requires non-manipulation)

**Actionable Framework — The Reversed Rules:**
1. "Put people to work. It's even more effective than money."
2. "Challenge your people to explore, learn, and get comfortable with uncertainty."
3. "Find ways to help others on the path find firm footing."
4. "Treat the others the way you'd want to be treated."
5. "Stick with your tactics long after everyone else is bored with them."
6. "Don't make threats. Do or don't do."
7. "Build a team with the capacity and patience to do the work that needs doing."
8. "Celebrate your people, free them to do even more."

**Reversal:** Manipulation works short-term but "tears away at civil discourse." Godin: "When you're so sure you're right that you're willing to burn things down, everyone is standing in a burning building sooner or later."

## 8. [TIM:PM] Permission Marketing

**Source:** This Is Marketing (Godin), Chapter 17
**Core Principle:** Permission marketing is the privilege of delivering anticipated, personal, and relevant messages to people who want to get them. Real permission means "if you stop showing up, people are concerned. They ask where you went." It's like dating — you earn the right over time. Permission is the most valuable marketing asset you can own. "Every publisher, every media company, every author of ideas needs to own a permission asset, the privilege of contacting people without a middleman."
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: Building any audience; before buying ads; when you want sustainable growth; designing your engagement strategy
- AVOID WHEN: You think buying an email list = permission (it doesn't); you're about to spam because of quarterly pressure
- COMBINES WITH: `[TIM:TT]` (permission is built on trust), `[TIM:SVA]` (permission from your SVA), `[TIM:FN]` (permission feeds the funnel)

**Key Examples:**
- **Yoyodyne (Godin's company):** Sending more email than anyone on the planet with 70%+ open rates and 33% response rates — all with active permission. "That's about a thousand times the rate of a typical commercial email sent in 2018." (Ch. 17)
- **DailyCandy:** City-focused email for young women. Permission asset sold for $100M+. (Ch. 17)
- **RapCaviar (Spotify):** Tuma Basa grew the playlist to 9 million subscribers. "The most important asset in the music business. Bigger than any radio station." (Ch. 17)
- **Facebook as sharecropping:** "You're a sharecropper. It's not your land. You don't have permission to contact people; they do. You don't own an asset; they do." (Ch. 17)

**Actionable Framework:**
1. Permission is anticipated (they expect it), personal (it's for them), relevant (it matches their needs)
2. "Just because you somehow get my email address doesn't mean you have permission"
3. Make a specific promise: "I will do X, Y, Z" — then that's ALL you do
4. Never sell, rent, or abuse the list. "Protect it. It's more valuable than the laptops or chairs in your office."
5. Own your permission asset — don't rent it from social platforms
6. Metric worth measuring: "How many people would complain if you didn't send that next email?"
7. Use permission to educate, drip by drip, message by message

**Reversal:** "If it sounds like you need humility and patience to do permission marketing, that's because it does. That's why so few companies do it properly." Permission is slow. It requires keeping promises. There are no shortcuts.

### [TIM:PM.1] Showing Up with Generosity

**Source:** This Is Marketing (Godin), Chapter 17
**Core Principle:** You earn permission by showing up generously where neophiliacs gather. Not with a sales pitch, but with genuine value. "It doesn't matter that there's only a tiny percentage of the movie market at Comic-Con. What matters is the quality of their story and the depth of their empathy and generosity."
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: Starting from zero permission; launching something new; looking for your first followers
- AVOID WHEN: You show up with a pitch instead of a gift
- COMBINES WITH: `[TIM:PM]` (parent), `[TIM:SVA]` (show up where your SVA gathers)

**Key Examples:**
- **Marvel at Comic-Con:** A year before a movie release, they show up with special previews, director interviews, actual news — not to sell tickets, but to earn permission. (Ch. 17)
- **Purple Cow concept:** Being remarkable isn't about stunts — it's about being worth talking about. "You do people a service when you make better things and make it easy to talk about them." (Ch. 17)

**Actionable Framework:**
1. Find where your neophiliacs gather
2. Show up with genuine value, not a sales pitch
3. Signal to the tribe that this is "the sort of thing people like us will be talking about"
4. Design for evangelism: "People aren't going to spread the word because it's important to you. They'll only do it because it's important to them."

**Reversal:** Stunts and offensive gimmicks are not generosity. "Offensive/juvenile/urgent/selfish is not the same thing as purple." Remarkable means worth remarking on.

### [TIM:PM.2] Own It, Don't Rent It

**Source:** This Is Marketing (Godin), Chapter 17
**Core Principle:** Social media platforms make it apparently easy to reach people, but "you're a sharecropper. It's not your land." Platforms charge you more over time and own the relationship. Build your own permission asset — email list, direct subscription, owned channels.
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: Your business depends on social media reach; you've been hit by algorithm changes; you need a durable marketing asset
- AVOID WHEN: You've already built owned channels and are using social only for discovery
- COMBINES WITH: `[TIM:PM]` (parent), `[TIM:FN]` (owned permission feeds owned funnels)

**Actionable Framework:**
1. "You should own that, not rent it"
2. Email lists, RSS feeds, direct subscriptions = owned
3. Social media followers, platform subscribers = rented
4. Use rented platforms to drive to owned channels
5. "If someone on your team spams the list just to make a metric go up" — fire them

**Reversal:** Owned permission assets require maintenance and investment. They're not passive. But they compound, unlike rented attention that can vanish overnight.
