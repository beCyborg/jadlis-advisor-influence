# Show Your Work! — 10 Principles Complete Reference

> Source: Show Your Work! 10 Ways to Share Your Creativity and Get Discovered (Austin Kleon, 2014)
> Tag prefix: `[SYW`

## 1. [SYW:P1] You Don't Have to Be a Genius

**Source:** Show Your Work! (Kleon), Chapter 1
**Core Principle:** Creativity is not a solo act of lone genius. The musician Brian Eno coined the term "scenius" — great ideas emerge from an ecology of talent: artists, curators, thinkers, and tastemakers who support each other, look at each other's work, copy from each other, and contribute ideas. The Internet makes joining a scenius easier than ever — you don't need credentials, money, or fame. Being a valuable contributor is about what you share, the quality of connections you make, and the conversations you start.
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: You feel like an impostor; you think you need to be an expert before sharing; you're starting something new; you want to join a community
- AVOID WHEN: You're using "scenius" as an excuse to never develop individual craft; you're leeching value without contributing
- COMBINES WITH: `[SYW:P6]` (teaching as contribution to scenius), `[SYW:P7]` (being a good citizen, not human spam), `[SYW:P3]` (daily contributions to the scene)

---

### [SYW:P1.1] Embrace Scenius

**Source:** Show Your Work! (Kleon), Chapter 1
**Core Principle:** The "lone genius" myth is destructive. Creativity is always collaboration — a mind connected to other minds. Scenius acknowledges that good work isn't created in a vacuum. The Internet is "a bunch of sceniuses connected together, divorced from physical geography." Blogs, social media, forums — they're all virtual scenes where people discuss what they care about. There's no bouncer, no gatekeeper.
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: You're building in public; you want to join an existing community; you need collaborators; you're in a field with an active online scene
- AVOID WHEN: The community is toxic; you're substituting community participation for actual work
- COMBINES WITH: `[SYW:P7.2]` (finding your fellow knuckleballers), `[SYW:P4]` (sharing influences connects you to scene)
**Key Examples:**
- Brian Eno coined "scenius" — many "lone geniuses" throughout history were actually part of a whole scene of people supporting each other
- The Internet levels the playing field — artist and curator, master and apprentice, expert and amateur can all contribute
**Actionable Framework:**
1. Identify 2-3 online sceniuses relevant to your work (communities, forums, social platforms)
2. Observe what people are sharing and what they're NOT sharing
3. Look for voids you can fill with your own efforts, no matter how rough
4. Contribute consistently — ideas, feedback, support, your own work-in-progress
5. Focus on what you can do for others, not what others can do for you
**Reversal:** Scenius is not a shortcut for hard work. Participating in a scene without producing work makes you a spectator, not a contributor. The scene amplifies what you create — it doesn't replace creation.

### [SYW:P1.2] Be an Amateur

**Source:** Show Your Work! (Kleon), Chapter 1
**Core Principle:** The word "amateur" comes from the French for "lover" — one who pursues work in the spirit of love. Amateurs have advantages over professionals: they're willing to try anything, take chances, experiment, make mistakes in public. "In the beginner's mind, there are many possibilities. In the expert's mind, there are few" (Shunryu Suzuki). Amateurs learn in the open so others can learn from their failures and successes.
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: You're new to a field; you're afraid of looking stupid; you feel you need more expertise before sharing; the world is changing fast and everyone's becoming an amateur
- AVOID WHEN: You're in a domain where amateur errors have serious consequences (medicine, engineering safety); you're presenting yourself as an expert when you're not
- COMBINES WITH: `[SYW:P6]` (amateurs as teachers — "fellow-pupil can help more than the master"), `[SYW:P2]` (sharing the learning process), `[SYW:P10.3]` (becoming a student again)
**Key Examples:**
- Charlie Chaplin: "That's all any of us are: amateurs. We don't live long enough to be anything else."
- Sex Pistols effect: Bernard Sumner saw them, they were terrible, and he wanted to get up and be terrible with them. Raw enthusiasm is contagious.
- Thom Yorke (Radiohead) picks up instruments he doesn't know to stay in beginner's mind
- C.S. Lewis: "The fellow-pupil can help more than the master because he knows less. The difficulty we want him to explain is one he has recently met."
**Actionable Framework:**
1. Commit to learning something in front of others
2. Share what you love — people who love the same things will find you
3. Wear your amateurism on your sleeve — your heart, your love
4. Don't wait for permission or credentials — contribute something
5. Accept that mediocrity is on the spectrum of creative work; doing something beats doing nothing
**Reversal:** "Embrace the amateur" is not "stay mediocre forever." The point is to start sharing before you're ready, while continuously improving. Use the amateur phase as a launchpad, not a permanent residence.

### [SYW:P1.3] Find Your Voice by Using It

**Source:** Show Your Work! (Kleon), Chapter 1
**Core Principle:** "The only way to find your voice is to use it. It's hardwired, built into you. Talk about the things you love. Your voice will follow." The example of Roger Ebert, who lost his physical voice to cancer, then found his "real voice" through blogging, tweeting, and posting online — writing thousands of words about everything, connecting with hundreds of people daily.
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: You're paralyzed about "finding your voice" before starting; you want to wait until you have a unique perspective
- AVOID WHEN: You're producing content purely for content's sake without genuine interest; you're copying someone else's voice rather than developing your own
- COMBINES WITH: `[SYW:P3]` (daily dispatch develops voice through practice), `[SYW:P5]` (stories shape voice)
**Key Examples:**
- Roger Ebert: Cut off from speaking by cancer, blogged at breakneck speed, found his "real voice" online. "Mr. Ebert writes as if it were a matter of life and death — because it is."
- Kleon: "if your work isn't online, it doesn't exist"
**Actionable Framework:**
1. Stop waiting to find your voice — start using it now
2. Talk about the things you love — voice emerges from passion
3. Post regularly — voice develops through practice, not introspection
4. Accept that your early voice will be rough — it gets refined through use
**Reversal:** Using your voice doesn't mean shouting. It means expressing what matters to you consistently. A voice that says nothing meaningful, no matter how often it speaks, won't resonate.

### [SYW:P1.4] Read Obituaries (Memento Mori)

**Source:** Show Your Work! (Kleon), Chapter 1
**Core Principle:** Thinking about death puts everything in perspective. Kleon reads obituaries every morning — "obituaries are like near-death experiences for cowards." They remind him that people before him started as amateurs, got where they were going by having guts to put themselves out there. The Steve Jobs quote: "Remembering that I'll be dead soon is the most important tool I've ever encountered."
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: You're procrastinating on sharing; you feel you have something to lose; fear of judgment is paralyzing you
- AVOID WHEN: Anxiety or existential dread is already overwhelming; it becomes morbid rather than motivating
- COMBINES WITH: `[SYW:P1.2]` (amateurs with guts), `[SYW:P3]` (urgency drives daily sharing)
**Actionable Framework:**
1. Read obituaries regularly — notice how people lived, not how they died
2. Use mortality awareness as motivation to share now, not tomorrow
3. Ask: "If I only had a year left, would I keep this work private?"
4. Remember George Lucas after his car accident: "Every day now is an extra day"
**Reversal:** Memento mori should inspire action, not paralysis or recklessness. The goal is perspective, not anxiety.

## 2. [SYW:P2] Think Process, Not Product

**Source:** Show Your Work! (Kleon), Chapter 2
**Core Principle:** There are two kinds of work: the artwork (finished product) and the art work (the day-to-day process behind the scenes). Traditionally artists kept their process secret, but in the digital age, sharing process creates a unique bond with the audience. "People really do want to see how the sausage gets made." By letting go of ego and sharing process, you allow for ongoing connection with people and your work.
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: You have nothing finished to show; you're in the apprentice stage; your products aren't easily shared; you want to build relationship with audience
- AVOID WHEN: Your process contains genuinely proprietary/secret methods; sharing process would compromise the final product's impact (some art relies on mystery)
- COMBINES WITH: `[SYW:P3]` (daily dispatch IS the process shared), `[SYW:P5]` (stories about process), `[SYW:P6]` (teaching your process)

---

### [SYW:P2.1] Share the Behind-the-Scenes

**Source:** Show Your Work! (Kleon), Chapter 2
**Core Principle:** Thanks to the Internet and social media, an artist can share whatever she wants at almost no cost. She can share sketches, works-in-progress, pictures of her studio, blog about influences, inspiration, and tools. Audiences want to stumble across great work AND be part of the creative process. As Brene Brown says: "In order for connection to happen, we have to allow ourselves to be seen — really seen."
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: Building audience before you have a product; wanting to humanize your brand; creating anticipation for something coming; building trust through transparency
- AVOID WHEN: Over-sharing personal life (this is not your diary); sharing things you wouldn't want everyone to see
- COMBINES WITH: `[SYW:P5.1]` (structure your behind-the-scenes as stories), `[SYW:P4]` (share your influences as part of process)
**Key Examples:**
- Chris Hadfield (ISS commander): Tweeted, posted photos of Earth, filmed videos of clipping nails, brushing teeth, sleeping, doing maintenance in space. Millions watched him do plumbing repair — because IT'S IN SPACE!
- Edgar Allan Poe (1846): Writers "would positively shudder at letting the public take a peep behind the scenes" — the old mindset Kleon is challenging
- Dan Provost & Tom Gerhardt: "By putting things out there, consistently, you can form a relationship with your customers. It allows them to see the person behind the products."
**Actionable Framework:**
1. Identify what stage of your process you're in (early/middle/late — this determines what to share)
2. Early stage: share influences and inspirations
3. Middle stage: share methods, works in progress
4. Late stage: share final product, cutting-room scraps, lessons learned
5. Post-launch: share how people are interacting with your work
**Reversal:** Process-sharing can become procrastination if you spend more time documenting than doing. The process comes first; sharing it is secondary. Set a timer: 30 minutes for sharing, then back to work.

### [SYW:P2.2] Become a Documentarian

**Source:** Show Your Work! (Kleon), Chapter 2
**Core Principle:** "Become a documentarian of what you do." Turn the invisible into something other people can see. Start a work journal, keep a scrapbook, take photographs at different stages, shoot video. This isn't about making art — it's about keeping track of what's going on around you. When you're ready to share, you'll have a surplus of material to choose from. Documenting has its own rewards even unshared: you see your work more clearly and feel like you're making progress.
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: You feel you have nothing to show; you want a stockpile of content; you want to understand your own work better
- AVOID WHEN: Documentation is consuming more time than the actual work; you're documenting obsessively without ever sharing
- COMBINES WITH: `[SYW:P3]` (documentation feeds daily dispatch), `[SYW:P3.3]` (documentation becomes your stock over time)
**Key Examples:**
- David Carr: "No one is going to give a damn about your resume; they want to see what you have made with your own little fingers."
- Kleon's advice: Use cheap tools — most of us carry a "fully functional multimedia studio" in our smartphones
**Actionable Framework:**
1. Start a work journal (notebook or digital)
2. Photograph your work at different stages
3. Record audio notes or video snippets of process
4. Keep a scrapbook of inspiration and influences
5. Revisit documentation regularly — it feeds future content
**Reversal:** Documentation without sharing is a private archive, not a creative practice. At some point, you must curate and publish. Also, don't confuse documentation with work — the work comes first.

## 3. [SYW:P3] Share Something Small Every Day

**Source:** Show Your Work! (Kleon), Chapter 3
**Core Principle:** "Overnight success is a myth." Building a substantial body of work takes a lifetime, but you can focus on days — the only unit of time Kleon can "get his head around." Once a day, after your day's work, find one little piece of your process to share. A daily dispatch is better than a resume or portfolio because it shows what you're working on right now. The form doesn't matter — blog post, tweet, video, email. Social media sites are the perfect place for daily updates.
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: You want to build audience momentum; you have regular work to document; you want to stay top-of-mind with your audience
- AVOID WHEN: You have nothing genuine to share (don't force it — quality over quantity); sharing is taking precedence over actual work
- COMBINES WITH: `[SYW:P2]` (process provides daily material), `[SYW:P5]` (each dispatch tells a micro-story), `[SYW:P1.3]` (daily sharing develops voice)

---

### [SYW:P3.1] The Daily Dispatch

**Source:** Show Your Work! (Kleon), Chapter 3
**Core Principle:** The form of your daily dispatch doesn't matter — it can be a blog post, email, tweet, YouTube video, or any bit of media. There's no one-size-fits-all plan. What matters is consistency and showing what you're working on right now. Like "getting all the DVD extras before a movie comes out." Pick platforms based on what you do and who you're trying to reach. Don't be afraid to try new platforms; abandon those that don't work.
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: You have a regular creative practice; you want to build a daily sharing habit; you need to choose which platforms to focus on
- AVOID WHEN: You're spreading too thin across too many platforms; daily posting feels forced (try weekly instead)
- COMBINES WITH: `[SYW:P3.2]` (the "So What?" filter for each dispatch), `[SYW:P4]` (dispatches can share influences, not just own work)
**Key Examples:**
- Ze Frank (interviewing job candidates): "When I ask them to show me work, they show me things from school, or from another job, but I'm more interested in what they did last weekend."
- Bobby Solomon: "Put yourself, and your work, out there every day, and you'll start meeting some amazing people."
- Dribbble.com's prompt: "What are you working on?" — stick to that question and you'll be good
- Tommy Edison (blind since birth): Takes photos of daily life, posts to Instagram, 30,000+ followers
**Actionable Framework:**
1. Choose 1-2 primary platforms based on your audience and medium
2. After each day's work, find one piece to share (influence, method, WIP, final product, lesson)
3. Use the prompt "What are you working on?" as your filter
4. Find time in the cracks — commute, lunch break, after kids' bedtime
5. Set a 30-minute timer for sharing; kick yourself off the Internet when it goes off
6. Don't let sharing take precedence over doing the work
**Reversal:** Daily sharing should never become the work itself. If you're spending more time crafting posts than doing your actual work, recalibrate. The dispatch documents the work — it doesn't replace it.

### [SYW:P3.2] The "So What?" Test

**Source:** Show Your Work! (Kleon), Chapter 3
**Core Principle:** Not everything should be shared. Before posting, run everything through the "So What?" Test: "Is this helpful? Is it entertaining? Is it something I'd be comfortable with my boss or my mother seeing?" If unsure, let it sit for 24 hours. "The save as draft button is like a prophylactic — it might not feel as good in the moment, but you'll be glad you used it in the morning." Remember: sharing is an act of generosity — you're putting something out because you think it might help or entertain someone.
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: Every single time before posting anything; when you're emotional or unsure; when the content is personal or edgy
- AVOID WHEN: Never skip this test. The test itself takes seconds.
- COMBINES WITH: `[SYW:P7]` (the generous intent check — are you giving value or just self-promoting?), `[SYW:P5]` (good stories pass the test naturally)
**Key Examples:**
- Kleon's college professor: Threw chalk, wrote "SO WHAT?" in huge letters on the blackboard after returning graded essays
- Dani Shapiro: "Make no mistake: This is not your diary. You are not letting it all hang out. You are picking and choosing every single word."
- Kevin Kelly: "The Internet is a copy machine" — once posted, content never leaves. Post as though everyone who can read it has the power to fire you.
**Actionable Framework:**
1. Before posting, ask: "So what?" — is this helpful or entertaining?
2. If unsure, save as draft. Revisit with fresh eyes in 24 hours.
3. Check: Would I be comfortable with my boss or mother seeing this?
4. Share generously, but distinguish between sharing and over-sharing
5. "Post as though everyone who can read it has the power to fire you" (Lauren Cerand)
**Reversal:** Overthinking the test can paralyze you. "Don't overthink it; just go with your gut." The test is a quick filter, not a lengthy deliberation. If everything gets stuck in drafts forever, your bar is too high.

### [SYW:P3.3] Stock and Flow

**Source:** Show Your Work! (Kleon), Chapter 3
**Core Principle:** Robin Sloan's metaphor adapted from economics: "Flow is the feed — the posts and tweets, the stream of daily and sub-daily updates that remind people you exist. Stock is the durable stuff — the content you produce that's as interesting in two months (or two years) as it is today. It's what people discover via search." The magic formula: maintain your flow while working on your stock in the background. Your stock is best made by collecting, organizing, and expanding upon your flow.
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: You want a sustainable content strategy; you want to turn daily output into something lasting; you're deciding between ephemeral and durable content
- AVOID WHEN: You're only producing flow without ever consolidating; you're only producing stock without staying visible
- COMBINES WITH: `[SYW:P3.1]` (daily dispatch = flow), `[SYW:P3.4]` (your own website = stock container), `[SYW:P2.2]` (documentation becomes stock)
**Key Examples:**
- Kleon's own book: Ideas started as tweets → became blog posts → became book chapters. "Small things, over time, can get big."
- Social media sites as "public notebooks" — places to think out loud, let others think back, then think some more
**Actionable Framework:**
1. Maintain daily flow (social media posts, tweets, short updates)
2. Revisit your flow regularly — look for themes and patterns
3. When you detect patterns, gather bits into something bigger (blog post, guide, course, book)
4. Your blog turns flow into stock: "One little blog post is nothing on its own, but publish a thousand over a decade, and it turns into your life's work"
5. Think of flow as raw material and stock as refined product
**Reversal:** All flow, no stock = you're always starting over, never building. All stock, no flow = you're invisible between major releases. The balance between the two is the sustainable creative practice.

### [SYW:P3.4] Build Your Own Website

**Source:** Show Your Work! (Kleon), Chapter 3
**Core Principle:** Social networks come and go ("Remember Myspace? Friendster? GeoCities?"), but owning your own space online means a world headquarters no one can take away. Kleon bought austinkleon.com over 10 years ago, started with a bare bones ugly website, installed a blog, and "absolutely everything good that has happened in my career can be traced back to my blog." A website is a self-invention machine, not a self-promotion machine. Patti Smith got this advice from William Burroughs: "Build a good name. Keep your name clean... eventually that name will be its own currency."
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: You're serious about long-term creative career; you want a platform you control; you want to build stock that compounds over time
- AVOID WHEN: You don't have time to maintain it (but even a minimal presence is better than none)
- COMBINES WITH: `[SYW:P3.3]` (website is the ideal flow-to-stock machine), `[SYW:P9.3]` (email list runs from your own domain)
**Key Examples:**
- Kleon: Blog was his "sketchbook, studio, gallery, storefront, and salon"
- Andy Baio: "Carving out a space for yourself online... is still one of the best possible investments you can make with your time."
- Patti Smith / William Burroughs: "Build a good name. Keep your name clean. Don't make compromises."
**Actionable Framework:**
1. Register a domain name (your name or a meaningful alias)
2. Set up a website — it doesn't have to be pretty, it just has to exist
3. Install a blog — the ideal machine for turning flow into stock
4. Think of it as a self-invention machine, not self-promotion
5. Don't abandon it for the newest social network — think long-term
6. Let it change with you over time
**Reversal:** A website without content or updates is a ghost town. The site is only valuable if you use it. Don't spend months perfecting design before publishing — start ugly and iterate.

## 4. [SYW:P4] Open Up Your Cabinet of Curiosities

**Source:** Show Your Work! (Kleon), Chapter 4
**Core Principle:** In 16th-17th century Europe, wealthy collectors kept "Wunderkammern" (cabinets of curiosities) — rooms of rare objects displaying their thirst for knowledge. Today, your influences, tastes, and collections ARE your cabinet of curiosities. "Our tastes make us what we are." Sharing what you love — your inspirations, reading list, influences — clues people in to who you are, sometimes even more than your own work. Before you're ready to share your own work, you can share your tastes in the work of others.
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: You don't have your own work to share yet; you want to signal taste and identity; you want to attract like-minded people; you're building credibility in a new field
- AVOID WHEN: You're only sharing others' work and never creating your own (balance is key); you're curating without attribution
- COMBINES WITH: `[SYW:P1.1]` (contributing to scenius by sharing influences), `[SYW:P4.2]` (always credit properly), `[SYW:P7]` (sharing influences = connector behavior, not spam)

---

### [SYW:P4.1] Share Your Influences and Tastes

**Source:** Show Your Work! (Kleon), Chapter 4
**Core Principle:** Where do you get your inspiration? What do you read, watch, listen to, collect? Who do you steal ideas from? "Your influences are all worth sharing because they clue people in to who you are and what you do." Don't be ashamed of guilty pleasures — "I don't believe in guilty pleasures. If you f---ing like something, like it" (Dave Grohl). Be open about what you like, including "low" culture and "garbage." What makes you unique is the breadth of your influences and how you mix high and low.
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: You want to build authenticity; you want to connect with people who share your tastes; you're in early stages and don't have much original work yet
- AVOID WHEN: You're hiding behind others' work to avoid creating your own; the influences you share don't represent who you really are (curating for image, not truth)
- COMBINES WITH: `[SYW:P5]` (tell stories about WHY these things matter to you), `[SYW:P2]` (influences are part of your process), `[SYW:P4.2]` (always credit)
**Key Examples:**
- Nelson Molina (NYC trashman): Built "The Trash Museum" on the second floor of a Sanitation Department garage — 1000+ objects found in trash. "Treasure in the trash."
- Ira Glass: "We get into creative work because we have good taste." Before your work matches your taste, share the taste itself.
- DJ Spooky: "You're only as good as your record collection."
- Jonathan Lethem (writer/former bookseller): "I'm basically a curator. Making books has always felt very connected to my bookselling experience."
**Actionable Framework:**
1. Make a list: What inspires you? What do you read, watch, collect?
2. Share books, articles, music, films, tools — things that shape your work
3. Don't self-edit for coolness — own all of it, including "guilty pleasures"
4. Explain WHY each thing matters to you (story + attribution)
5. Share regularly as part of your flow — your cabinet is always evolving
6. "Do what you do best and link to the rest" (Jeff Jarvis)
**Reversal:** Sharing influences without ever creating original work turns you into a curator, not a creator. The cabinet should enrich your creative identity, not replace it. Also, always credit (see P4.2).

---

### [SYW:P4.2] Credit Is Attribution

**Source:** Show Your Work! (Kleon), Chapter 4
**Core Principle:** When sharing others' work, proper credit is your duty. Without attribution, you rob the creator AND your audience (they can't find more of that work). Attribution means providing context: what the work is, who made it, how, when, where, why you're sharing it, and where to find more. Online, the most important form is a hyperlink back to the source. "If you don't include a link, no one can click it." If you can't find the proper credit — don't share.
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: Always. Every time you share someone else's work.
- AVOID WHEN: Never skip attribution.
- COMBINES WITH: `[SYW:P7]` (proper credit is connector behavior), `[SYW:P1.1]` (crediting builds scenius)
**Key Examples:**
- Kleon follows "via" and "H/T" (hat tip) links and has discovered many interesting people through meticulous attribution chains
- "Attribution is about putting little museum labels next to the stuff you share"
**Actionable Framework:**
1. Always include: who made it, what it is, why it matters, link to source
2. Include "via" or "H/T" to credit where YOU found it
3. If you can't find the credit — don't share
4. "99.9 percent of people are not going to bother Googling someone's name" — always link
5. Treat shared work with the same respect and care as your own
**Reversal:** Over-attribution can clutter your message. Keep credit clean and functional. But when in doubt, over-credit rather than under-credit.

## 5. [SYW:P5] Tell Good Stories

**Source:** Show Your Work! (Kleon), Chapter 5
**Core Principle:** "Our work doesn't speak for itself." Human beings want to know where things came from, how they were made, and who made them. The stories you tell about your work have a huge effect on how people feel about it, understand it, and value it. Every email, text, tweet, photo, video is a bit of a multimedia narrative you're constantly constructing. To be more effective when sharing, you need to become a better storyteller.
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: You're presenting work to an audience; you're pitching; you're explaining what you do; you want to increase perceived value of your work
- AVOID WHEN: You're inventing fiction rather than telling truth; your stories obscure rather than illuminate the work
- COMBINES WITH: `[SYW:P2]` (process stories), `[SYW:P5.2]` (pitch structure for unfinished work), `[SYW:P1.3]` (stories develop your voice)

---

### [SYW:P5.1] Use Story Structure

**Source:** Show Your Work! (Kleon), Chapter 5
**Core Principle:** A good story structure is tidy, sturdy, and logical. John Gardner's basic plot: "A character wants something, goes after it despite opposition, and so arrives at a win, lose, or draw." This maps perfectly to creative work: get an idea → work hard to execute → release into the world → win, lose, or draw. The Pixar formula (Emma Coats): "Once upon a time, there was ___. Every day, ___. One day, ___. Because of that, ___. Because of that, ___. Until finally, ___."
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: Writing about completed projects; crafting case studies; telling origin stories; presenting results
- AVOID WHEN: Work is still in progress (use the pitch structure instead — see P5.2)
- COMBINES WITH: `[SYW:P5.2]` (pitch = story with ending chopped off), `[SYW:P2]` (process provides the middle act)
**Key Examples:**
- Art forgery experiment: Identical paintings — one by a Dutch master, one by a student. Knowing the story changes which one you want. "Our work doesn't speak for itself."
- Significant Objects experiment: $128.74 of trinkets sold for $3,612.51 on eBay by attaching invented stories. Stories are a measurable driver of emotional value.
- Psychology professor Paul Bloom: "People's assessment — how much they like it, how valuable it is — is deeply affected by what you tell them about it."
**Actionable Framework:**
1. Identify your three acts: Problem (beginning) → Work (middle) → Result (end)
2. Use the Pixar formula as a template for project stories
3. Include the human element — frustrations, turning points, personal stakes
4. Keep it in plain language. Be brief. Value your audience's time.
5. Study great stories, then find your own. Skill improves with practice.
**Reversal:** Don't force a neat story onto messy reality. Sometimes honest uncertainty is more compelling than a polished narrative. And never fabricate — "stick to nonfiction."

---

### [SYW:P5.2] The Pitch (Stories with Endings Chopped Off)

**Source:** Show Your Work! (Kleon), Chapter 5
**Core Principle:** Most of us are in the middle of a story and don't know the ending. Every pitch — client presentation, cover letter, fundraising request — is a story with the ending chopped off. Three-act pitch structure: Past (where you've been, what you want, how you came to want it), Present (where you are now, how you've used resources), Future (where you're going, how the listener can help). This "Choose Your Own Adventure" structure turns the listener into the hero who decides how it ends.
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: Work is in progress; fundraising; seeking collaborators; job interviews; "What do you do?" situations
- AVOID WHEN: The work is complete (use full story structure instead)
- COMBINES WITH: `[SYW:P5.3]` (bio as compressed pitch), `[SYW:P9.2]` (crowdfunding pitch)
**Key Examples:**
- Every client presentation, cover letter, personal essay, fund-raising request — they're all pitches
- The "Choose Your Own Adventure" effect: listener becomes the hero
**Actionable Framework:**
1. Act 1 (Past): What you want + how you came to want it + what you've done so far
2. Act 2 (Present): Where you are now + hard work invested + resources used
3. Act 3 (Future): Where you're going + how the listener can help
4. Practice: elevator pitch, one-paragraph version, full version
5. Tailor to audience — buddies at the bar vs. your mother vs. a potential investor
**Reversal:** A pitch that's all future and no past/present feels like vaporware. A pitch that's all past feels like a resume. Balance all three acts.

### [SYW:P5.3] Your Bio as Story

**Source:** Show Your Work! (Kleon), Chapter 5
**Core Principle:** "So, what do you do?" — the dreaded question. The way to handle it: stop treating it as interrogation, start treating it as an opportunity to connect by honestly explaining what you do. Bios are not the place for creativity — keep them short and sweet. Strike adjectives from your bio. If you take photos, you're a photographer — not "aspiring" or "amazing." Don't use ninja, guru, or rock star unless you literally are one.
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: Writing social media bios; answering "What do you do?"; conference introductions
- AVOID WHEN: You need a detailed CV (different format)
- COMBINES WITH: `[SYW:P5.1]` (bio is the shortest story), `[SYW:P1.2]` (be honest about amateur status)
**Key Examples:**
- Kleon for years: "By day I'm a web designer, and by night I write poetry."
- Saul Steinberg: "I'm a writer who draws." (Kleon stole this format)
- George Orwell: "Autobiography is only to be trusted when it reveals something disgraceful."
**Actionable Framework:**
1. State facts. Strip adjectives. Two sentences max.
2. If you have a hybrid role, describe it honestly ("writer who draws")
3. Be ready for follow-up questions — answer patiently and politely
4. If your title feels wrong, examine why — maybe you're in the wrong work, or not doing the right work
5. Never use: ninja, guru, rock star, aspiring, amazing
**Reversal:** Being too humble or cryptic in your bio is as bad as bragging. People need to understand what you do. Clarity over cleverness.

## 6. [SYW:P6] Teach What You Know

**Source:** Show Your Work! (Kleon), Chapter 6
**Core Principle:** "The impulse to keep to yourself what you have learned is not only shameful, it is destructive. Anything you do not give freely and abundantly becomes lost to you. You open your safe and find ashes" (Annie Dillard). Teaching doesn't mean instant competition — just because someone knows your technique doesn't mean they can emulate it. Teaching adds value to your work by generating more interest and letting people in on what you know. And you receive an education in return.
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: You've learned something valuable; you have a unique process or technique; you want to deepen understanding of your own work; you want to attract people interested in your field
- AVOID WHEN: You're teaching what you don't actually know; the teaching is a substitute for doing the work
- COMBINES WITH: `[SYW:P1.1]` (teaching strengthens scenius), `[SYW:P1.2]` (amateurs can teach what they're learning), `[SYW:P2]` (process is what you teach)

### [SYW:P6.1] Share Your Secrets

**Source:** Show Your Work! (Kleon), Chapter 6
**Core Principle:** Even in secretive, competitive fields, the best practitioners share their knowledge. Teaching doesn't create instant competition because mastery requires years of practice beyond knowing the technique. The Franklins share everything about barbecue because "there's an intuition that you only gain through the repetition of practice." Jason Fried and David Heinemeier Hansson (Rework): businesses should "out-teach their competition."
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: You have hard-won knowledge; your field is competitive and secretive (bigger impact when you break the norm); you want to build trust and authority
- AVOID WHEN: Sharing would genuinely harm your business (rare — usually fear, not reality); you're teaching without personal experience
- COMBINES WITH: `[SYW:P3]` (teach daily in small bits), `[SYW:P4]` (share your tools, references, influences as teaching), `[SYW:P9]` (teaching builds audience for eventual monetization)
**Key Examples:**
- Aaron Franklin (Franklin Barbecue): Best BBQ in America, teaches everything on YouTube — how to modify a smoker, select wood, build a fire, select meat, what temperature, how to slice. Still sells out every day because mastery takes years.
- Kathy Sierra: "Make people better at something they want to be better at."
- Christopher Hitchens: Publishing a book brought contact with "people whose opinions you should have canvassed before you ever pressed pen to paper." Sharing work = "free education that goes on for a lifetime."
- Fried & Hansson (Rework): "What do you do? What are your 'recipes'? What's your 'cookbook'?"
**Actionable Framework:**
1. Think about what you can share that would inform people you're trying to reach
2. The minute you learn something, turn around and teach it to others
3. Share your reading list, helpful references, tutorials
4. Use pictures, words, video — take people step-by-step through your process
5. Figure out the equivalent of your own "cooking show"
6. Remember: teaching adds value, it doesn't subtract it
**Reversal:** Teaching without doing becomes armchair expertise. The credibility of your teaching comes from your practice. If your teaching outpaces your doing, rebalance. Also, teaching is not consulting — freely share knowledge; charge for implementation.

## 7. [SYW:P7] Don't Turn Into Human Spam

**Source:** Show Your Work! (Kleon), Chapter 7
**Core Principle:** "Human spam" = people who don't want to pay their dues, who want their piece right now, who don't want to listen, only want to tell you their ideas, who don't go to shows but thrust flyers at you to come to theirs. The antidote: "If you want fans, you have to be a fan first. If you want to be accepted by a community, you have to first be a good citizen of that community." Shut up and listen. Be an open node. Be a connector.
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: Always. This is the ethical backbone of all sharing principles.
- AVOID WHEN: Never. There is no situation where being human spam is acceptable.
- COMBINES WITH: `[SYW:P1.1]` (scenius requires good citizenship), `[SYW:P4]` (sharing others' work = connector), `[SYW:P6]` (teaching = giving value, not spam)

### [SYW:P7.1] Be an Open Node (Give Before You Get)

**Source:** Show Your Work! (Kleon), Chapter 7
**Core Principle:** "If you're only pointing to your own stuff online, you're doing it wrong." Blake Butler calls this being an "open node." If you want to get, you have to give. If you want to be noticed, you have to notice. Forward-thinking artists aren't looking for passive consumers — they're looking for collaborators, co-conspirators. Quality of followers matters more than quantity. "Follow me back?" is the saddest question on the Internet.
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: Building your online presence; engaging with communities; wanting meaningful connections vs. vanity metrics
- AVOID WHEN: Your "giving" is actually veiled self-promotion; you're giving so much you have no time for your own work
- COMBINES WITH: `[SYW:P4]` (sharing others' work is open-node behavior), `[SYW:P1.1]` (contributing to scenius), `[SYW:P7.3]` (meeting online friends IRL)
**Key Examples:**
- Adrian Younge: Tweeted "Who is better: The Dramatics or The Delfonics?" — led to a collaboration with the lead singer of The Delfonics on a new album. A single tweet, but born from genuine fan engagement.
- Donald Barthelme to a student: "Have you tried making yourself a more interesting person?" To be interesting, you have to be interested.
- Steve Albini: "Connections don't mean shit. Being good at things is the only thing that earns you clout or connections."
- Jeffrey Zeldman: "This thing is about hearts and minds, not eyeballs."
**Actionable Framework:**
1. Share others' work generously — be a connector, not just a broadcaster
2. Ask for recommendations, not followers
3. Talk about stuff you love, not just stuff you made
4. Quality over quantity: don't follow people strategically, follow people you genuinely find interesting
5. Don't be creepy, don't be a jerk, don't waste people's time
6. "Make stuff you love and talk about stuff you love and you'll attract people who love that kind of stuff."
**Reversal:** Being an open node doesn't mean having no boundaries. You can give generously while still protecting your time and energy. The goal is genuine connection, not self-sacrifice.

### [SYW:P7.2] The Vampire Test

**Source:** Show Your Work! (Kleon), Chapter 7
**Core Principle:** Pablo Picasso was notorious for sucking energy from people — his granddaughter said "he squeezed people like one of his tubes of oil paints." The Romanian sculptor Brancusi refused to engage with Picasso because he recognized a vampire. The test: After hanging out with someone, do you feel worn out and depleted (vampire) or still full of energy (not a vampire)? Apply this test to people, jobs, hobbies, and places. "Vampires cannot be cured."
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: Evaluating relationships, collaborations, projects, communities; feeling drained by certain interactions; deciding whom to let into your creative life
- AVOID WHEN: Confusing normal creative challenge or productive friction with vampirism (discomfort isn't always drainage)
- COMBINES WITH: `[SYW:P7.1]` (choose your open-node connections wisely), `[SYW:P10]` (sustainability requires removing vampires)
**Key Examples:**
- Picasso squeezed people's energy, then painted all night with it. Not a sustainable relationship for anyone but Picasso.
- Brancusi: "knew a vampire when he saw one" — simply refused to engage
- Derek Sivers: "Whatever excites you, go do it. Whatever drains you, stop doing it."
**Actionable Framework:**
1. After interactions, check your energy level — up or down?
2. Apply to people, jobs, hobbies, projects, and online communities
3. If vampire: banish from your life (there is no cure)
4. If not vampire: nurture the relationship
5. Apply to your own behavior too — are YOU a vampire to others?
**Reversal:** Not everything that's hard is a vampire. Growth, learning, and creative friction can be uncomfortable but energizing in hindsight. The test is about net energy over time, not momentary discomfort.

### [SYW:P7.3] Find Your Knuckleballers (and Meet IRL)

**Source:** Show Your Work! (Kleon), Chapter 7
**Core Principle:** Knuckleball pitchers in baseball are rare, so they form a brotherhood and share tips freely. "Knuckleballers don't keep secrets. It's as if we have a greater mission beyond our own fortunes. And that mission is to pass it on." Your fellow knuckleballers are your real peers — people who share your obsessions and mission. There'll only be a handful, but they're invaluable. Nurture these relationships. And turn online friends into IRL friends — "there's never any small talk — we know all about one another."
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: You've found people who share your obsessions; you want deeper creative relationships; you have online connections worth deepening
- AVOID WHEN: You're trying to force peer relationships with people who don't share your actual interests; you're substituting online community for all human contact
- COMBINES WITH: `[SYW:P1.1]` (knuckleballers are your personal scenius), `[SYW:P6]` (share secrets with peers), `[SYW:P7.1]` (open-node behavior attracts peers)
**Key Examples:**
- R.A. Dickey and fellow knuckleball pitchers: "There's no chance that an opposing pitcher is going to invite me to watch how he grips his slider. Those are state secrets. With knuckleballers, things are different."
- Kleon on meetups: "There's never any small talk — we know all about one another." Online friends meeting IRL is the best part of being online.
- Susan Sontag: "It's all about paying attention. Attention is vitality. It connects you with others."
**Actionable Framework:**
1. As you share your work, notice who consistently engages — these are potential knuckleballers
2. Sing their praises, invite them to collaborate, show them work before anyone else
3. Share secrets with peers — reciprocal trust deepens relationships
4. Meet online friends IRL — suggest coffee, lunch, or attend meetups
5. Keep your circle of real peers small and tight — handful is enough
**Reversal:** Don't become so insular that you only talk to your tiny circle. Knuckleballers are your inner ring, but the broader scenius matters too.

## 8. [SYW:P8] Learn to Take a Punch

**Source:** Show Your Work! (Kleon), Chapter 8
**Core Principle:** "The more people come across your work, the more criticism you'll face." Designer Mike Monteiro says the most valuable skill from art school was learning to take a punch during brutal critiques. The approach: relax and breathe (fear is imagination taking a wrong turn), strengthen your neck (put out more work), roll with punches (every criticism is opportunity for new work), protect vulnerable areas (keep too-sensitive work hidden), keep your balance (your work is something you do, not who you are).
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: You've received criticism; you're afraid of putting work out; trolls are getting to you; you feel personally attacked by feedback
- AVOID WHEN: The "criticism" is actually valuable feedback you should incorporate; you're using "taking a punch" as an excuse to ignore all feedback
- COMBINES WITH: `[SYW:P8.2]` (distinguishing trolls from legitimate critics), `[SYW:P3]` (more output = more practice taking punches), `[SYW:P10]` (persistence requires resilience)

### [SYW:P8.1] How to Take Punches

**Source:** Show Your Work! (Kleon), Chapter 8
**Core Principle:** Five practical strategies for handling criticism: (1) Relax and breathe — no one has died from a bad review. (2) Strengthen your neck — the more criticism you take, the more you realize it can't hurt you. (3) Roll with the punches — push further into what people hate. (4) Protect your vulnerable areas — keep too-sensitive work hidden. (5) Keep your balance — your work is something you do, not who you are.
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: After receiving any negative feedback or criticism
- AVOID WHEN: The feedback reveals a genuine flaw you need to fix
- COMBINES WITH: `[SYW:P3]` (high output volume makes any single criticism less significant), `[SYW:P10.1]` (chain-smoking = always moving forward past criticism)
**Key Examples:**
- Cyndi Lauper: "I come pre-hated. Take your best shot."
- Brian Michael Bendis: "The trick is not caring what EVERYBODY thinks of you and just caring about what the RIGHT people think of you."
- Colin Marshall: "Compulsive avoidance of embarrassment is a form of suicide."
- Mike Monteiro: Art school critiques taught him not to take criticism personally
**Actionable Framework:**
1. **Relax and breathe.** Bad criticism is not the end of the world. Consider meditation.
2. **Strengthen your neck.** Put out more work. Volume builds resilience.
3. **Roll with the punches.** Turn criticism into fuel for new work.
4. **Protect your vulnerable areas.** Keep genuinely sensitive work private.
5. **Keep your balance.** Stay close to family, friends, people who love you for you.
**Reversal:** "Taking punches" doesn't mean becoming numb. Stay open to legitimate feedback from people who care about you and your work. Resilience is not indifference.

### [SYW:P8.2] Dealing with Trolls

**Source:** Show Your Work! (Kleon), Chapter 8
**Core Principle:** A troll wants to provoke, not to improve your work. "Don't feed them, and they'll usually go away." The worst troll is the one in your head — the voice saying you're not good enough. Use the block button. Delete nasty comments. "If someone took a dump in your living room, you wouldn't let it sit there, would you?" Consider turning off comments completely: "There's never a space under paintings in a gallery where someone writes their opinion."
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: Someone is provoking rather than constructively criticizing; you feel drained by online interactions; your inner critic is amplified by external negativity
- AVOID WHEN: The critic has a legitimate point, even if delivered poorly; you're labeling everyone who disagrees as a "troll"
- COMBINES WITH: `[SYW:P7.2]` (vampire test for online relationships), `[SYW:P8.1]` (broader resilience framework)
**Key Examples:**
- The mother who sent Kleon tweets after his son's birth, quoting passages from Steal Like an Artist and mocking them — "Ha! Try that when you're up at three a.m. with a crying baby!" Got to him because it amplified his own internal troll.
- Natalie Dee (cartoonist): "When you get to the end of a book, you don't have to see what everyone else thought of it."
**Actionable Framework:**
1. First evaluate: Is this person trying to improve my work or provoke me?
2. If troll: block, delete, do not engage
3. If internal troll amplified: recognize the mechanism — external trolls are megaphones for self-doubt
4. Consider reducing comment surfaces (turn off blog comments, limit DMs)
5. Let people copy your work to their own spaces and discuss it there
**Reversal:** Blocking everyone who disagrees creates an echo chamber. Be honest about whether you're blocking trolls or avoiding legitimate criticism.

## 9. [SYW:P9] Sell Out

**Source:** Show Your Work! (Kleon), Chapter 9
**Core Principle:** "People need to eat and pay the rent." Get over the "starving artist" romanticism — money doesn't inherently corrupt creativity. Michelangelo was commissioned by the pope. Lennon and McCartney would sit down and say "Now, let's write a swimming pool." The word "sellout" is spit out by the bitterest parts of ourselves. Don't write off friends for having success. If an opportunity means more of the kind of work you want to do — say Yes. If it means more money but less of the work — say No.
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: You're wondering whether to monetize your work; you feel guilt about charging; you're evaluating an opportunity; someone accuses you of "selling out"
- AVOID WHEN: Money is driving you away from the work you love (the one legitimate "sellout" concern); you haven't built enough audience to monetize yet
- COMBINES WITH: `[SYW:P9.1]` (ways to make money), `[SYW:P9.3]` (email list as monetization channel), `[SYW:P7]` (monetization should give value, not be spam)

---

### [SYW:P9.1] Ways to Monetize

**Source:** Show Your Work! (Kleon), Chapter 9
**Core Principle:** Multiple paths: (1) Virtual tip jar / donate button ("Like this? Buy me a coffee."). (2) Crowdfunding platforms (Kickstarter, Indiegogo) — work best when you already have a gathered audience. (3) Traditional sales: "buy now" and "hire me" buttons. The leap to asking for money should come when you're confident you're putting out work truly worth something. "Don't be afraid to charge for your work, but put a price on it that you think is fair."
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: Audience is gathering around your free work; you have something worth paying for; you're confident in your value
- AVOID WHEN: You haven't built trust yet (monetizing too early kills trust); crowdfunding without an existing audience
- COMBINES WITH: `[SYW:P9.3]` (email list = monetization infrastructure), `[SYW:P5.2]` (pitch structure for crowdfunding), `[SYW:P3]` (free daily sharing builds the audience you later monetize)
**Key Examples:**
- Amanda Palmer: After years of showing work, sharing music freely, and cultivating relationships — asked for $100K on Kickstarter, got $1M+.
- John T. Unger (street poet): Someone says "Your poem changed my life!" — "Want to buy a book? $5." — "Nah, that's okay." — "Geez, how much is your life worth?"
- Kleon's model: Make something, sell it. "Buy now" and "hire me" buttons instead of donations.
- Bill Withers: "We're all entrepreneurs. The best sign you can put up is sold out."
**Actionable Framework:**
1. Start with free sharing to build audience and trust
2. When audience gathers, test simple monetization (tip jar, small product)
3. For bigger projects, crowdfund only if you already have a gathered audience
4. Or keep it traditional: make things, sell things
5. Price fairly — not too low (devalues work), not too high (limits access)
6. Take the monetization leap only when confident your work is truly worth something
**Reversal:** Monetizing too early, before value is established, feels like spam. Monetizing too late means you starve. Find the sweet spot when audience trust is built but before you burn out working for free.

### [SYW:P9.2] Say Yes to Opportunity, No to Distraction

**Source:** Show Your Work! (Kleon), Chapter 9
**Core Principle:** Dave Eggers: "I really like saying yes. I like new things, projects, plans, getting people together and doing something." Be ambitious. Keep busy. Think bigger. Expand your audience. If an opportunity lets you do more of the work you love — say Yes. If it means more money but less of your work — say No. But beware: "The biggest problem of success is that the world conspires to stop you doing the thing that you do" (Neil Gaiman). At some point, switch from saying "yes" a lot to saying "no" a lot.
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: Evaluating opportunities, collaborations, or pivots; success is creating distractions; you need a decision framework for saying yes/no
- AVOID WHEN: Using "say yes" as an excuse to avoid focus; using "say no" as an excuse to avoid growth
- COMBINES WITH: `[SYW:P7.2]` (vampire test for opportunities), `[SYW:P10]` (sustainability requires protecting your creative time)
**Key Examples:**
- John Coltrane: "The real risk is in not changing. If I make money, fine. But I'd rather be striving."
- Neil Gaiman: Realized he'd "become someone who professionally replied to email, and who wrote as a hobby"
- Kleon's office hours: Monthly open Q&A to manage email volume while staying generous
- Walt Disney: "We don't make movies to make money, we make money to make more movies."
**Actionable Framework:**
1. Decision filter: Does this opportunity let me do MORE of the work I love? → Yes. More money but LESS of my work? → No.
2. Early career: default to Yes (try new things, expand)
3. Later career: default to No (protect creative time)
4. Use office hours or batch responses to stay generous without drowning in email
5. "Be generous enough, but selfish enough to get your work done."
**Reversal:** "Say yes to everything" burns you out. "Say no to everything" isolates you. The filter is always: what serves the work?

### [SYW:P9.3] Build Your Email List

**Source:** Show Your Work! (Kleon), Chapter 9
**Core Principle:** "Even though almost everybody hates it, everybody has an email address. And unlike RSS and social media feeds, if you send someone an email, it will land in her inbox." Email is decades old and nowhere close to dead — the boring utilitarian technologies stick around longest. The model: give away great stuff on your site, collect emails, then when you have something to share or sell, send an email. "You'd be amazed at how well the model works." Never add someone's email without permission.
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: You have a website; you want a direct line to your audience that doesn't depend on platform algorithms; you plan to monetize eventually
- AVOID WHEN: You have nothing to offer subscribers yet (build value first); you're going to spam them
- COMBINES WITH: `[SYW:P3.4]` (email list lives on your own website), `[SYW:P9.1]` (email list is the monetization engine), `[SYW:P7]` (treat the list with respect — don't be spam)
**Key Examples:**
- "I know people who run multimillion-dollar businesses off of their mailing lists"
- The model: give away great stuff → collect emails → send when you have something remarkable
**Actionable Framework:**
1. Put a sign-up widget on every page of your website
2. Write clear copy: what will they get? (daily, monthly, infrequent updates)
3. Never add someone without permission
4. Treat your list with respect — these are your biggest supporters
5. Send only when you have something genuinely worth sharing or selling
6. The list is your insurance against platform changes
**Reversal:** A list you never email becomes stale. A list you email too often becomes spam. Find a rhythm that delivers genuine value.

### [SYW:P9.4] Pay It Forward

**Source:** Show Your Work! (Kleon), Chapter 9
**Core Principle:** When you achieve success, use your dough, clout, and platform to help people who helped you get there. Extol teachers, mentors, heroes, influences, peers, and fans. Give them chances to share their own work. Throw opportunities their way. "Above all, recognize that if you have had success, you have also had luck — and with luck comes obligation" (Michael Lewis).
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: You've achieved any level of success or platform; you have influence to share; someone helped you along the way
- AVOID WHEN: You're "paying it forward" as a calculated self-promotion tactic (it should be genuine)
- COMBINES WITH: `[SYW:P1.1]` (strengthening the scenius), `[SYW:P7.1]` (open-node behavior), `[SYW:P4.2]` (crediting those who influenced you)
**Actionable Framework:**
1. Publicly credit mentors, teachers, influences
2. Share others' work with your audience
3. Open doors for people coming up behind you
4. Give back to the communities that supported you
5. Do it genuinely, not as a strategy
**Reversal:** Don't over-commit to helping others at the expense of your own work. "Be generous enough, but selfish enough to get your work done."

## 10. [SYW:P10] Stick Around

**Source:** Show Your Work! (Kleon), Chapter 10
**Core Principle:** "The people who get what they're after are very often the ones who just stick around long enough." Careers have ups and downs — you never know where you are in the story. "If you want a happy ending, that depends, of course, on where you stop your story" (Orson Welles). There are second, third, fourth, and fifth acts. The key: don't quit prematurely. "You gotta play till the ninth inning, man."
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: You feel like quitting; you just had a failure; you're burned out; you're comparing yourself to overnight success stories
- AVOID WHEN: You've genuinely exhausted an idea and need to pivot (persistence ≠ stubbornness); the work is making you miserable with no redeeming qualities
- COMBINES WITH: `[SYW:P10.1]` (chain-smoking for momentum), `[SYW:P10.2]` (sabbaticals for renewal), `[SYW:P10.3]` (beginning again for freshness)

---

### [SYW:P10.1] Chain-Smoking (Never Lose Momentum)

**Source:** Show Your Work! (Kleon), Chapter 10
**Core Principle:** Avoid stalling by using the end of one project to light up the next. Don't take breaks between projects waiting for feedback. Ask: "What did I miss? What could I have done better? What couldn't I get to?" — then jump into the next project. Lifelong artists share this pattern: Woody Allen (a film a year for 40+ years), Bob Pollard (never stops writing), Hemingway (stopped mid-sentence to know where to start next morning), Joni Mitchell (the weak link in her last project inspires the next).
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: You just finished a project; you're in the lull between projects; you tend to stall after completing work
- AVOID WHEN: You're burning out (see P10.2); you need time to process feedback before starting something new
- COMBINES WITH: `[SYW:P3]` (daily habit maintains momentum between projects), `[SYW:P8]` (don't let criticism stop your chain)
**Key Examples:**
- Woody Allen: Averages a film a year for 40+ years. The day he finishes editing is the day he starts writing the next script.
- Ernest Hemingway: Stopped writing mid-sentence at end of day so he knew where to start in the morning.
- Joni Mitchell: Whatever she feels is the weak link in her last project gives her inspiration for the next.
- Bob Pollard (Guided by Voices): Never gets writer's block because he never stops writing.
- Charles Eames: "We work because it's a chain reaction, each subject leads to the next."
**Actionable Framework:**
1. Before finishing a project, note what you missed, what you'd do better, what you couldn't get to
2. Use those notes as the spark for the next project
3. Don't wait for feedback or reception — start the next thing immediately
4. Keep a running list of "next projects" so you always have something to jump to
5. "Just do the work that's in front of you"
**Reversal:** Chain-smoking without reflection produces quantity without growth. Build in brief moments of reflection (not paralysis) between projects. Chain-smoking is about momentum, not mindlessness.

---

### [SYW:P10.2] Take Sabbaticals (Practical Ones)

**Source:** Show Your Work! (Kleon), Chapter 10
**Core Principle:** Stefan Sagmeister shuts down his studio every seven years for a year off — "Everything that we designed in the seven years following the first sabbatical had its roots in thinking done during that sabbatical." Most of us can't take a full year, but we can take practical sabbaticals — daily, weekly, or monthly breaks. Three prime disconnection spots: commute (write, read, stare out the window), exercise (relaxed body opens mind), nature (disconnect from electronics). "If you never go to work, you never get to leave work."
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: You feel burned out; creativity is drying up; you can't seem to generate new ideas; you've been chain-smoking too long
- AVOID WHEN: You're using "sabbatical" as an excuse to avoid work; you haven't done enough work yet to need a break
- COMBINES WITH: `[SYW:P10.1]` (chain-smoking needs periodic sabbaticals to prevent burnout), `[SYW:P10.3]` (sabbaticals create space for beginning again)
**Key Examples:**
- Stefan Sagmeister: 7-year cycle, year off. Required 2 years of planning and budgeting.
- Kleon: First two years out of college, worked part-time in a library, read and wrote and drew. Spent years since executing ideas from that period.
- Gina Trapani's three disconnection spots: commute, exercise, nature
- Robert Mitchum: "Every two or three years, I knock off for a while. That way, I'm constantly the new girl in the whorehouse."
**Actionable Framework:**
1. Build micro-sabbaticals into your routine: commute, exercise, nature walks
2. Separate work from the rest of life — have clear boundaries
3. Disconnect from electronics regularly
4. Plan periodic larger breaks (weekend, week, month)
5. Use sabbaticals for recharging and inspiration, not catching up on work
6. "If you never go to work, you never get to leave work"
**Reversal:** A sabbatical that turns into permanent vacation means you've quit, not recharged. Set a return date. Use the break intentionally — not just for rest, but for replenishment.

### [SYW:P10.3] Begin Again (Become a Student)

**Source:** Show Your Work! (Kleon), Chapter 10
**Core Principle:** "When you feel like you've learned whatever there is to learn from what you're doing, it's time to change course and find something new to learn." Anyone who isn't embarrassed of who they were last year probably isn't learning enough (Alain de Botton). Louis C.K. threw out his material every year after learning George Carlin did the same — "When you get rid of old material, you push yourself further and come up with something better." You never really start over — the lessons seep into what you do next. Go back to chapter one and become an amateur again.
**Decision Algorithm:**
- APPLY WHEN: You've mastered something and feel stale; your work is getting too comfortable; you want to grow past a plateau
- AVOID WHEN: You're abandoning things before mastering them (shiny object syndrome); you're throwing away working approaches that still serve you
- COMBINES WITH: `[SYW:P1.2]` (return to amateur mindset), `[SYW:P2]` (document the new learning process), `[SYW:P3]` (share the new journey daily)
**Key Examples:**
- Louis C.K.: Threw out his hour of material (developed over 15 years!) after learning George Carlin did it annually. "You can only dig deeper."
- Thom Yorke: Picks up instruments he doesn't know to write with
- Milton Glaser: "Whenever Picasso learned how to do something, he abandoned it."
- Steven Soderbergh: "I need to tear down everything I've done and rebuild from scratch."
- Alain de Botton: "Anyone who isn't embarrassed of who they were last year probably isn't learning enough."
**Actionable Framework:**
1. Periodically ask: Am I still learning? Is this still challenging?
2. If work feels too comfortable or stale, find something new to learn
3. Commit to learning the new thing in the open — document and share
4. Don't think of it as starting over — lessons from the past seep into the future
5. "Show your work, and when the right people show up, pay close attention to them, because they'll have a lot to show you"
**Reversal:** Beginning again doesn't mean discarding expertise. It means staying a student. Mastery is a cycle: learn → practice → master → find new thing to learn → repeat. The danger is never completing the cycle — always beginning, never mastering.
